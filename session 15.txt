Comprehensive Revision Notes: An Introduction to Derivatives
Overview
In this session, the class explored the fundamentals of financial derivatives as instruments in the financial markets. The focus was on understanding what derivatives are, how they derive value from underlying assets, and their various types and uses in risk management and speculation. Key concepts such as options, futures, and hedging strategies were discussed, providing learners with a foundational understanding of trading and risk mitigation.

What are Derivatives?
Derivative: A financial instrument whose value depends on the value of an underlying asset.
Underlying Asset: Can be stocks, indices (e.g., Sensex or Nifty), commodities, or currencies.
Purpose: Used for speculation and risk management.
Why Use Derivatives?
Speculation: Allows investors to make profits based on the expected future price movements of the underlying asset.
Risk Management: Helps in hedging against potential losses in other investments. For instance, airlines hedge fuel price risks, and banks may hedge interest rate risks.
Key Characteristics of Derivatives
Underlying Asset: The asset from which a derivative's value is derived.
Strike/Exercise Price: The price at which an option is exercised.
Expiry Date: Derivatives have a predetermined expiry date unlike stocks.
Lot Size/Contract Size: Derivatives are traded in specific lot sizes or contract sizes.
Margin Requirements: Traders must maintain a margin account with a minimum balance.
Exchange vs. OTC: Derivatives can be traded on exchanges or over-the-counter (OTC) through private contracts.
Types of Derivative Contracts
Futures: A standardized contract to buy or sell an asset at a future date at an agreed-upon price.
Options: Contracts that offer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specified price before expiration.
Call Option: The right to buy.
Put Option: The right to sell.
Forwards: Customized contracts between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified future date.
Swaps: Contracts to exchange cash flows or liabilities from two different financial instruments.
Examples and Analogies
Example: Options
Consider owning a stock currently priced at ₹1000. A put option with a strike price of ₹800 can limit the loss if the price falls.
In the Money (ITM): The current price is favorable relative to the strike price.
Out of the Money (OTM): The strike price is not favorable when compared to the current market price.
Hedging Example
A farmer producing wheat and a mill buying wheat can enter a forward contract to fix the price at ₹25,000 per ton, protecting both from future price volatility.
Risk and Reward in Options
Buyer:
Maximum Profit: Unlimited (when market price exceeds strike price significantly).
Maximum Loss: Limited to the premium paid.
Seller:
Maximum Profit: Limited to the premium received.
Maximum Loss: Potentially unlimited (if the market moves significantly against the position).
Analogies in Real Life
Exercise of an option is likened to deciding whether to attend a class. You have the right but not the obligation to attend, similar to holding a put option.
Conclusion
This class detailed the essential role of derivatives in financial markets, highlighting their utility in speculation and risk management. By understanding derivatives' characteristics, types, and applications, investors and financial professionals can efficiently navigate market risks. This foundational knowledge is critical for anyone looking to explore more advanced financial instruments and their implications in real-world financial strategies.
