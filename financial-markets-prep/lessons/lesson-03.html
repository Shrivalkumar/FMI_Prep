Cost Concepts, Costing & CVP

Financial accounting looks backwards and reports to outsiders. Management accounting looks forward and helps managers inside the business plan, control and decide. This lesson builds that toolkit from zero — how we define and classify costs, how overheads get into a product's cost, the difference between marginal and absorption costing, and the one framework that ties it all together: Cost–Volume–Profit (CVP).

In this lesson

1. Management vs financial accounting

You already met financial accounting in Lesson 1 — the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement prepared for investors, lenders and regulators under GAAP. Management accounting uses much of the same raw data, but points it in a completely different direction: at the people running the firm.

BasisFinancial accountingManagement accounting
UsersInvestors, lenders, regulators (external)Managers inside the firm (internal)
RulesGAAP & accounting standards; auditedNo fixed rules — built for the decision
Time viewPast resultsFuture-oriented plans & options
DetailThe whole companyProducts, departments, projects, customers

Management accounting does three jobs, which are worth memorising as a trio:

Planning

Estimate costs, revenues, profit and resources ahead of time — i.e. build budgets.

Control

Compare actual with budget and study the variance. Budget ₹50L, actual ₹54L → a ₹4L adverse variance to investigate.

Decisions

Make-or-buy, outsource-or-produce, product choice, and pricing (cost-plus, penetration, skimming, marginal-cost).

💡 Exam tip Sessions 5–8

If a question says "which figures would a manager use to choose between two options next year?", it is a management-accounting question — think future, relevant and decision-focused, not GAAP.

2. Cost concepts & classification

Before we can analyse costs we need the vocabulary. Two words students mix up constantly:

TermMeaningExample
CostSacrifice of resources to achieve an objective₹10L paid for a machine
ExpenseA cost charged against revenue in a period₹1.8L yearly depreciation of that machine
Cost objectAnything we measure cost forA product, project, customer or department
Cost unitThe unit in which cost is expressedPer car, per room-night, per kWh, per patient-day

So the ₹10L machine is a cost the day you buy it; each year, ₹1.8L of it becomes an expense through depreciation. And unit cost = total production cost ÷ units: ₹50,00,000 ÷ 10,000 units = ₹500 a unit. Airlines express it as cost per passenger-kilometre; transport firms as cost per tonne-kilometre.

The four lenses of cost classification

The same rupee of cost can be sliced four different ways, depending on the question you are asking. Picture a car factory:

Classify byCategoriesCar factory example
NatureMaterial, labour, other expensesSteel, assembly wages, rent
BehaviourFixed, variable, semi-variableFactory rent vs steel vs the power bill
TraceabilityDirect, indirect (overhead)Steel body vs factory lubricant
FunctionProduction, admin, selling, distributionPlant cost vs showroom cost

By behaviour — the most important lens for CVP

How does a cost react when you make more units? This split is the engine of everything later in the lesson.

TypeExampleAs volume rises…
FixedFactory rent ₹10L a monthTotal stays flat; per unit falls — ₹1,000/car at 1,000 cars, ₹100/car at 10,000
VariableRaw material ₹20 per bottleTotal rises with output; per unit stays ₹20 (₹20,000 at 1,000 bottles → ₹2,00,000 at 10,000)
Semi-variablePower: ₹50,000 fixed + ₹8/machine-hourHas both parts — ₹58/hour at 1,000 hours, ₹13/hour at 10,000
The key insight

Fixed cost per unit falls as you spread it over more units; variable cost per unit stays constant. That is exactly why volume changes profit, and why break-even exists.

By traceability & by function

Direct costs can be traced to one cost unit (the steel body and the welder's wages on one car). Indirect costs — a.k.a. overheads — cannot: factory rent, electricity, insurance, the security guard, the supervisor. By function, costs split into production (making it), administration (running the firm), selling (winning sales) and distribution (getting goods to customers). This matters because only production costs become product (inventory) cost under absorption costing; admin, selling and distribution are period costs, written off as incurred.

By relevance — the lens for decisions

When choosing between options, most costs are noise. Only relevant costs matter.

Relevant cost

A future cost that differs between the options. Only these belong in a decision.

Irrelevant cost

A cost that is the same whichever option you pick — so it cannot swing the decision.

Sunk cost

Already spent and unrecoverable. ₹2 crore spent building software is ignored when deciding to launch or abandon it. It is history.

Opportunity cost

The benefit of the best alternative you give up. Choosing an MBA over a ₹16 lakh job costs ₹16 lakh of forgone salary.

⚠️ Classic trap

A sunk cost feels relevant ("we've already put ₹2 crore in, we can't stop now!"). It is not. The money is gone either way — compare only the future cost and revenue of launching versus abandoning.

3. Overhead absorption & full product cost

Direct costs are easy: you can trace them straight to a unit. Overheads are the hard part — how much of the factory rent, power, depreciation, supervisor pay and security belongs to one motor? We solve this by spreading overheads over output using an Overhead Absorption Rate (OAR):

Overhead absorption rate
$$\text{OAR} = \dfrac{\text{Total overhead}}{\text{Activity base}}$$

The activity base can be units, direct labour hours or machine hours — whatever best drives the overhead. Once we have the rate per unit, we build the full product cost:

$$\text{Full product cost} = \text{Direct material} + \text{Direct labour} + \text{Absorbed overhead}$$
Worked example A motor: direct ₹6,000, overhead ₹10,00,000 over 10,000 motors ›

A motor uses ₹4,000 direct material + ₹2,000 direct labour = ₹6,000 direct. The factory also incurs ₹10,00,000 of production overhead and plans to make 10,000 motors. It sells each motor for ₹7,500.

Formula OAR = Total overhead ÷ activity base.
Working — OAR = ₹10,00,000 ÷ 10,000 motors = ₹100 per motor.
StepWorkingResult
Overhead absorption rate₹10,00,000 ÷ 10,000 motors₹100 / motor
Full product cost₹4,000 + ₹2,000 + ₹100₹6,100
Profit at ₹7,500 price₹7,500 − ₹6,100₹1,400 / motor
Full cost ₹6,100 → profit ₹1,400 a motor.

Interpretation: every motor now carries ₹100 of factory overhead, so we can value stock, set prices and compare products on a fair footing.

Under- and over-absorption

We set the OAR using a forecast. At year-end the actual overhead rarely matches what was absorbed, so we must adjust:

  • Under-absorbed — actual overhead > absorbed. We didn't charge enough to products → it reduces profit.
  • Over-absorbed — actual overhead < absorbed. We charged too much → it increases profit.
The rule
Under/over-absorption = Actual overhead − Overhead absorbed  →  positive = under-absorbed (profit falls)

In the motor example, suppose actual overhead turns out to be ₹10,50,000 while only ₹10,00,000 was absorbed (₹100 × 10,000). The difference of ₹50,000 is under-absorbed and is written off in the income statement, cutting profit by ₹50,000.

ℹ️ Smaller illustration

Same idea, different numbers: overhead ₹1,00,000 ÷ 20,000 units = ₹5 absorbed into every unit. If 20,000 units are made, ₹1,00,000 is absorbed — exactly matching. The gap only appears when actual activity or actual overhead differs from plan.

4. Marginal vs absorption costing

Marginal cost is the extra cost of producing one more unit — in practice, the variable cost per unit, because fixed costs don't move when you make one extra.

Worked example Finding marginal cost from a step up in output ›
IllustrationWorkingResult
Output rises 10,000 → 10,100+100 units+100 units
Total cost rises ₹50,00,000 → ₹50,60,000+₹60,000+₹60,000
Marginal cost per component₹60,000 ÷ 100₹600
Marginal cost = ₹600 per component.

Fixed costs stayed put, so the entire ₹60,000 increase is variable. A ₹900 component therefore earns ₹900 − ₹600 = ₹300 of contribution.

Production 10,000 units, sales 8,000 at ₹500. Variable cost ₹300/unit; fixed production overhead ₹10,00,000 (= ₹100/unit at 10,000 units); selling & admin ₹2,00,000.

Interpretation: the ₹2,00,000 gap is ₹100 of fixed overhead sitting on each of the 2,000 unsold units, carried forward in closing stock under absorption costing instead of being expensed now. When production > sales (stock rises), absorption profit > marginal profit.

Contribution — the star of marginal costing

Contribution
$$\text{Contribution} = \text{Selling price} - \text{Variable (marginal) cost}$$

Contribution is what each unit "contributes" first to covering fixed costs, and then to profit. It drives short-run decisions.

Worked example The empty airline seat ›

A seat normally sells for ₹8,000. The aircraft lease, crew and airport charges are already committed (fixed). The only extra cost of carrying one more passenger:

Meal₹500
Passenger service₹200
Processing₹100
Other variable cost₹200
Marginal cost₹1,000

A last-minute buyer offers ₹3,000. Contribution = ₹3,000 − ₹1,000 = ₹2,000.

With empty seats and no capacity constraint — sell it. ₹2,000 that would otherwise be ₹0.
Worked example The same seat with a capacity constraint ›

A 180-seat flight has sold 175 seats at ₹8,000, so 5 remain. A travel firm wants 10 seats at ₹3,000, but walk-up individuals would pay ₹7,000. Now seats are scarce.

QuestionWorkingAnswer
Contribution, travel firm₹3,000 − ₹1,000₹2,000 / seat
Contribution, individual₹7,000 − ₹1,000₹6,000 / seat
Opportunity cost of a seatContribution forgone₹6,000
Minimum acceptable price₹1,000 + ₹6,000₹7,000
Reject the ₹3,000 offer. Each seat sold to the travel firm loses ₹6,000 − ₹2,000 = ₹4,000 of contribution; for 5 seats that is ₹20,000 thrown away.

Interpretation: with spare capacity, any price above marginal cost adds value. With a constraint, the opportunity cost of the next-best use must be added — so the floor price jumps from ₹1,000 to ₹7,000.

5. Cost–Volume–Profit (CVP) analysis

CVP uses contribution to answer the questions managers care about most: How many units to break even? To hit a target profit? How far can sales fall before we lose money? It assumes costs split cleanly into fixed and variable, and that price and unit variable cost stay constant within the relevant range.

The core logic: contribution covers fixed costs first, then every further rupee of contribution is profit.

$$\text{Profit} = (\text{Units} \times \text{Contribution per unit}) - \text{Fixed cost}$$

The five CVP formulas

Contribution & P/V ratio
$$\text{Contribution} = SP - VC \qquad \text{P/V ratio} = \dfrac{\text{Contribution}}{\text{Sales}}$$
Break-even
$$\text{BEP (units)} = \dfrac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{Contribution per unit}} \qquad \text{BEP (value)} = \dfrac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{P/V ratio}}$$
Target profit & margin of safety
$$\text{Units for target} = \dfrac{\text{Fixed cost} + \text{Target profit}}{\text{Contribution per unit}} \qquad \text{MoS}\% = \dfrac{\text{Actual sales} - \text{BEP sales}}{\text{Actual sales}}$$
💡 The P/V ratio shortcut

The P/V (Profit–Volume) ratio is contribution as a fraction of sales. It is handy because Margin of safety in value = Profit ÷ P/V ratio, and break-even in value is just Fixed cost ÷ P/V ratio — no need to know the unit price.

Worked example ClassicShoes Pvt Ltd — the full CVP picture ›

Price ₹2,000 a pair; variable cost ₹1,200 (material ₹700, labour ₹300, overhead ₹200); fixed cost ₹24,00,000 a year. The firm expects to sell 4,500 pairs.

MeasureFormula → workingResult
Contribution per pairSP − VC = ₹2,000 − ₹1,200₹800
P/V ratio₹800 ÷ ₹2,00040%
Break-even (units)₹24,00,000 ÷ ₹8003,000 pairs
Break-even (value)₹24,00,000 ÷ 40%₹60,00,000
Profit at 4,500 pairs4,500 × ₹800 − ₹24,00,000₹12,00,000
Margin of safety4,500 − 3,000 = 1,500 pairs; 1,500 ÷ 4,500₹30,00,000; 33.3%
Break-even 3,000 pairs; profit ₹12,00,000 at 4,500 pairs; margin of safety 33.3%.

Interpretation: every pair beyond the 3,000th adds ₹800 straight to profit. Sales can fall 33.3% (from 4,500 to 3,000 pairs) before the firm dips into a loss — a comfortable cushion.

Worked example A cloud kitchen — break-even and target profit ›

Price ₹250 a meal; variable cost ₹160 (ingredients, packaging, delivery commission); fixed cost ₹6,00,000 a month. It serves 9,000 meals a month.

MeasureFormula → workingResult
Contribution₹250 − ₹160₹90 / meal
P/V ratio₹90 ÷ ₹25036%

Interpretation: the kitchen's margin of safety is thinner than ClassicShoes' (25.9% vs 33.3%), so it is more exposed to a dip in orders. To more than double profit it needs to lift volume from 9,000 to 11,667 meals — a concrete, testable target.

6. Try the break-even calculator

Theory sticks when you move the numbers yourself. The calculator below solves the full CVP picture — break-even units and value, contribution and P/V ratio. Change the inputs (try ClassicShoes: price ₹2,000, variable cost ₹1,200, fixed cost ₹24,00,000) and watch the break-even point move as you cut the price or push up fixed cost.

✎ Try it — break-even units. A snackbox brand sells each box at ₹300 with variable cost ₹180, and has fixed costs of ₹3,60,000 a month. How many boxes must it sell each month to break even?

Contribution = ₹300 − ₹180 = ₹120 per box. Break-even = Fixed cost ÷ contribution = ₹3,60,000 ÷ ₹120 = 3,000 boxes. Each box beyond the 3,000th adds ₹120 to profit.

✎ Try it — P/V ratio. A product sells for ₹500 with a variable cost of ₹350 per unit. What is its P/V ratio, as a percentage? (Enter just the number, e.g. 30 for 30%.)

Contribution = ₹500 − ₹350 = ₹150. P/V ratio = contribution ÷ sales = ₹150 ÷ ₹500 = 30%. For every ₹100 of sales, ₹30 is available to cover fixed costs and then add to profit.

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Management accounting is internal, future-focused and rule-free; it does planning, control and decisions.
  • Classify costs by nature, behaviour, traceability, function and relevance. For decisions, use only relevant (future, differing) costs — ignore sunk costs.
  • OAR = total overhead ÷ activity base; full cost = direct material + direct labour + absorbed overhead. Actual > absorbed = under-absorbed (profit falls).
  • Contribution = SP − variable cost. Absorption costing parks fixed overhead in stock; marginal costing writes it off — so profits differ when stock changes.
  • CVP: Break-even = Fixed ÷ contribution; P/V = contribution ÷ sales; MoS% = (actual − BEP) ÷ actual; target units = (fixed + target) ÷ contribution.
