Capital Budgeting

Should the firm spend ₹80 lakh on a robotic production line? Build a new solar plant or just retrofit the lights? Capital budgeting is the toolkit for answering exactly these "big, long-term spend" questions. It takes everything you learned about the time value of money and turns it into a clear accept-or-reject decision.

In this lesson

1. What capital budgeting is & the appraisal process

Capital budgeting (also called investment appraisal) is how a business decides which long-term projects to invest in — a new factory, a machine, a product line, an acquisition. These decisions share three features that make them special:

  • Large — they tie up a lot of money.
  • Long-lived — the cash comes back over several years.
  • Hard to reverse — once the machine is bought, you can't easily undo it.

Because the money goes out now but comes back over future years, we cannot just add up the rupees. A ₹25 lakh inflow in year 5 is worth far less than ₹25 lakh today. So every good technique discounts future cash flows back to today. The process runs in five steps:

StepWhat happensExample
1. IdentifySpot an investment opportunityA chance to automate a production line
2. Estimate cash flowsForecast the initial outlay and the yearly inflowsInvest ₹80L now; ₹25L a year for 5 years
3. AppraiseApply NPV, IRR, payback, PIDiscount the inflows at the cost of capital
4. Accept / rejectApply the decision ruleNPV > 0 and IRR > cost of capital → accept
5. MonitorTrack the project after it startsCheck actual cash flows vs the forecast
ℹ️ The cost of capital Session 8 · Speed Run

The cost of capital (also "required return" or "hurdle rate") is the minimum annual return the project must earn to be worthwhile — it reflects what the firm pays for its funds and the risk of the project. We use it as the discount rate r throughout this lesson. In the Bharat AutoParts example it is 12%.

We will meet four appraisal tools. NPV and IRR discount cash flows (they respect the time value of money); payback is a quick rule of thumb; the profitability index ranks projects when money is scarce.

2. Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV is the single most important tool in capital budgeting. The idea is simple: bring every future cash inflow back to today's value, add them up, and subtract what you paid to start the project.

Net Present Value
$$\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} \; - \; \text{Initial investment}$$

Here CFt is the cash flow in year t, r is the cost of capital, and n is the project's life. NPV measures the extra value in today's rupees the project creates beyond the required return.

The NPV decision rule

NPV > 0 → accept (the project adds value). NPV = 0 → indifferent (it exactly meets the required return). NPV < 0 → reject (it destroys value). For independent projects, NPVs simply add up.

The annuity shortcut

When the yearly inflow is the same every year (an annuity), you don't need to discount each year separately. Multiply the yearly cash flow by the annuity factor:

Annuity (present-value) factor for r and n years
$$AF = \dfrac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}$$

Then PV of inflows = yearly cash flow × AF. This is exactly what we use for Bharat AutoParts.

Worked example Bharat AutoParts robotic line ›
Formula NPV = (yearly cash flow × annuity factor) − initial investment.
Given Invest ₹80,00,000 today; cash inflow ₹25,00,000 a year for 5 years; cost of capital 12%.
Working — annuity factor $AF = \dfrac{1-1.12^{-5}}{0.12} = 3.6048$.
Working — PV of inflows ₹25,00,000 × 3.6048 = ₹90,11,938 ≈ ₹90.12L.
Working — NPV ₹90,11,938 − ₹80,00,000 = +₹10,11,938.
NPV = +₹10.12 lakh. It is positive, so accept the robotic line.
Interpretation After earning the full 12% the firm demands, the project still throws off about ₹10.12 lakh of extra value in today's rupees. That surplus is what makes it worth doing.
💡 Exam tip — never forget the minus sign

The PV of inflows (₹90.12L) is not the NPV. You must subtract the initial investment (₹80L). Forgetting to subtract the outlay is the most common NPV mistake in exams.

✎ Try it — compute an NPV. A packaging machine costs ₹50 lakh and returns ₹15 lakh a year for 5 years. The cost of capital is 10% (annuity factor for 10%, 5 years = 3.7908). What is the NPV, in ₹ lakh?

PV of inflows = 15 × 3.7908 = ₹56.86L. NPV = 56.86 − 50 = +₹6.86L. Positive, so accept the machine. (If you got ₹56.86L, you forgot to subtract the ₹50L outlay.)

ℹ️ Try the real numbers in the calculator

The NPV/IRR calculator below is live. Enter the Bharat AutoParts figures — initial investment ₹80,00,000, then ₹25,00,000 in each of years 1–5, with a discount rate of 12% — and confirm you get NPV ≈ +₹10.12 lakh and IRR ≈ 17%.

3. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The IRR is the discount rate at which a project's NPV becomes exactly zero. In other words, it is the project's own built-in annual rate of return.

IRR is the rate r that solves…
$$\sum_{t=1}^{n} \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} - \text{Initial investment} = 0$$
The IRR decision rule

Accept if IRR > cost of capital (the project earns more than the firm demands). Reject if IRR < cost of capital. At IRR = cost of capital, NPV = 0.

There is no neat formula to solve for IRR directly. You find it by trial and error: try a rate, compute NPV, and adjust. When NPV is positive, try a higher rate; when NPV is negative, try a lower one. Then interpolate between the two closest rates. (A spreadsheet's IRR() function does this for you.)

Worked example Bharat AutoParts — finding the IRR by interpolation ›
Given Invest ₹80L; ₹25L a year for 5 years. We already know NPV at 12% is +₹10.12L (positive), so the IRR must be higher than 12%.
Working — trial rates Recompute the PV of the ₹25L annuity at higher rates:
Discount ratePV of inflowsNPV (− ₹80L)

Working — interpolate NPV crosses zero between 17% and 18%. $IRR \approx 17\% + \dfrac{2{,}000}{2{,}000 + 1{,}82{,}070}\times 1\% \approx 17.01\%$.
IRR ≈ 17%. Since 17% > the 12% cost of capital, accept — the same verdict as NPV.
Interpretation The robotic line earns about 17% a year on the money invested, comfortably above the 12% the firm needs. Interpolation just draws a straight line between the +₹2,000 and −₹1.82L points to estimate where NPV would be exactly zero.

4. When NPV & IRR disagree

For a single project, NPV and IRR usually give the same accept/reject answer. But for mutually exclusive projects — where you can pick only one — they can rank the projects differently. Look at a firm choosing between a big solar plant and a small LED retrofit (cost of capital 10%):

Worked example Solar plant vs LED retrofit (mutually exclusive) ›
Given Only one project can be chosen; cost of capital 10%.

NPV+₹21,94,730+₹4,26,439
IRR17.2%28.5%
NPV picks the Solar plant (+₹21.95L vs +₹4.26L). IRR picks the LED retrofit (28.5% vs 17.2%). They disagree.
Interpretation LED has the higher percentage return, but on a tiny ₹10L base. Solar earns a slightly lower percentage but on a ₹1 crore base, so it creates about five times more actual rupee wealth. For mutually exclusive projects, let NPV decide — choose Solar. NPV measures rupees of value added; IRR only measures a rate.
⚠️ Common mistake — using IRR to rank different-sized projects

The classic error is picking the project with the higher IRR regardless of scale. A 50% return on ₹1 lakh (₹50k) is worth far less than a 20% return on ₹1 crore (₹20L). When projects differ in size, when they return cash at different times (the reinvestment assumption), or when a project has multiple IRRs (from sign changes in its cash flows), IRR can mislead. The three causes of NPV–IRR conflict are: scale, reinvestment assumption, and multiple IRRs. When in doubt, follow NPV.

5. Payback & discounted payback

The payback period is the simplest tool of all: how many years until the project's cash inflows add back up to the money you put in?

Simple payback (equal cash flows)
Payback period = Initial investment ÷ Annual cash flow

For Bharat AutoParts: ₹80L ÷ ₹25L = 3.2 years. The trouble is that plain payback treats a rupee in year 4 the same as a rupee today — it ignores the time value of money. The discounted payback period fixes half of that by discounting each year's cash flow first, then asking when the discounted cumulative total reaches the investment.

Worked example Bharat AutoParts — payback vs discounted payback ›
Given Invest ₹80L; ₹25L a year for 5 years; discount rate 12%.
YearCash flowCumulativeDiscounted @12%Cum. discounted
1₹25L₹25L₹22.32L₹22.32L
2₹25L₹50L₹19.93L₹42.25L
3₹25L₹75L₹17.79L₹60.05L
4₹25L₹100L₹15.89L₹75.93L
5₹25L₹125L₹14.19L₹90.12L
Working — simple payback After year 3 the firm has recovered ₹75L; it still needs ₹5L of the ₹80L. In year 4 it earns ₹25L. Payback = 3 + (5 ÷ 25) = 3.2 years.
Working — discounted payback After year 4 the discounted total is ₹75.93L; it still needs ₹4.07L of the ₹80L. Year 5 brings ₹14.19L discounted. Discounted payback = 4 + (4.07 ÷ 14.19) = 4.29 years.
Simple payback = 3.2 years; discounted payback = 4.29 years.
Interpretation Discounting always makes payback longer (early rupees shrink less than late ones), which is more honest. But even discounted payback still ignores all cash flows after recovery — the ₹14.19L in year 5 past the break-even point does not count. That is why payback is a supporting measure, never the final word.
⚠️ Limitations of payback

Simple payback ignores the time value of money and ignores all cash flows after the payback point, so it can favour a short, low-value project over a longer, high-value one. Use it for a quick liquidity check, but let NPV make the decision.

✎ Try it — compute a payback period. A delivery van fleet costs ₹90 lakh and brings in a steady ₹25 lakh a year. What is the simple payback period, in years?

Payback = ₹90L ÷ ₹25L = 3.6 years. (Discounted payback would be longer, because later rupees are worth less.)

6. Profitability index & capital rationing

What if you have several good (positive-NPV) projects but not enough budget to do them all? That is capital rationing. You want the biggest total NPV per rupee invested — and the profitability index (PI) measures exactly that.

Profitability Index
$$\text{PI} = \dfrac{\text{PV of inflows}}{\text{Initial investment}}$$
Reading the PI

PI > 1 means NPV is positive (accept). PI = 1 means NPV is zero. PI < 1 means NPV is negative (reject). Under a budget limit, rank projects by PI and fund down the list until the money runs out. For Bharat AutoParts: PI = 90.12 ÷ 80 = 1.13 (above 1, so NPV is positive).

Interpretation Ranking by PI squeezes the most value out of each scarce rupee. Picking the biggest single NPV (D) or just filling the budget (A + D) would leave money on the table. PI beats raw NPV precisely when capital is limited.
💡 Exam tip — which tool when?

NPV first (the value-maximising rule, and the tie-breaker for mutually exclusive projects). IRR and PI to support it — PI especially under capital rationing. Payback only as a quick liquidity/risk check. For Bharat AutoParts all four agree: NPV +₹10.12L, IRR ≈17%, payback 3.2 yrs, PI 1.13 → accept.

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • NPV = Σ CFt ÷ (1+r)t − investment; accept if NPV > 0. Always subtract the outlay.
  • For a level annuity, PV of inflows = cash flow × annuity factor (Bharat: 25 × 3.6048 = ₹90.12L → NPV +₹10.12L).
  • IRR is the rate where NPV = 0; accept if IRR > cost of capital. Found by trial/interpolation (Bharat ≈ 17%).
  • For mutually exclusive projects, NPV and IRR can conflict (scale, reinvestment, multiple IRRs) — let NPV decide.
  • Payback = investment ÷ annual cash flow; discounted payback is longer and fairer, but both ignore cash after recovery.
  • PI = PV of inflows ÷ investment; rank by PI under capital rationing. PI > 1 ⇔ NPV > 0.
