Accounting Foundations & the Three Statements

Accounting is the language of business. Before you can read a ratio, value a bond or discount a cash flow, you need to understand where the numbers come from. This lesson builds that base from zero.

In this lesson

1. Why accounting exists

Accounting records, classifies and summarises business transactions in money terms. Its job is to answer three questions for anyone with a stake in a business: How did we perform? What do we own and owe? Where did the cash go?

Different people need those answers for different reasons:

UserUses the statements to…Example
ManagementPlan, control costs, decideA retail chain picks the best store to expand
Shareholders / investorsMeasure return and growthReviewing quarterly and annual results
Lenders (banks)Check repayment abilityReading audited statements before a loan
SuppliersSet credit limitsChecking the position before extending credit
GovernmentVerify tax & complianceGST and income-tax filings

Internal users run the business; external users invest, lend, supply or regulate it. The output comes in several flavours — financial, management, cost, tax, audit and forensic accounting.

2. The accounting equation

Everything in accounting hangs off one identity that must hold true after every transaction:

The accounting equation
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

In plain words: every rupee of stuff the business controls (assets) was paid for either by outsiders (liabilities) or by the owners (equity). Rearranged, equity is simply the owners' residual claim — what's left after settling what you owe:

$$\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$$

Watch it stay balanced as Ravi opens a kirana (grocery) store — invests ₹5 lakh, borrows ₹2 lakh:

TransactionCashInventoryLoan (L)Equity
Invests ₹5L, borrows ₹2L7L–2L5L
Buys stock for ₹3L cash4L3L2L5L
Sells ₹1L stock for ₹1.5L cash5.5L2L2L5.5L

After every line, Assets (Cash + Inventory) = Liabilities (Loan) + Equity. The ₹0.5L profit on the sale flowed straight into equity.

The seven elements

Assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses, gains and losses. The last four all move equity: income and gains raise it; expenses and losses reduce it. So between two balance dates:

$$\Delta\text{Equity} = \text{Income} - \text{Expenses} + \text{Gains} - \text{Losses} \;(-\text{ drawings/dividends})$$

3. Assets, liabilities & equity

Assets — what the business owns

An economic resource the business owns or controls, expected to give future benefit.

Current (used up within ~12 months / one operating cycle): cash, bank, receivables, inventory, short-term investments, prepaid expenses.
Non-current: buildings, machinery, vehicles, long-term investments, certain intangibles.

Liabilities & equity — who funds the assets

A liability is an obligation to an outsider.
Current: payables, salaries/interest/taxes payable, short-term borrowings.
Non-current: long-term loans, bonds, long-term lease liabilities.

Equity is the owners' residual claim — capital invested + profits retained − losses − drawings/dividends.

Proprietorship: Equity = Owner's capital + Profit − Drawings.
Company: Equity = Share capital + Retained earnings + Other reserves.

💡 Exam tip

Drawings (cash the owner takes for personal use) reduce equity directly — they are not a business expense. Mixing the two is a classic trap.

4. Core assumptions & concepts

These are the "rules of the game" that make statements meaningful and comparable.

Five base assumptions

ConceptMeaningExample
Business entityThe business is separate from its ownerAmazon's books are not Bezos's personal wealth
Going concernThe business will keep operatingAn oven is valued at cost less wear, not scrap value
Money measurementRecord only what is measurable in moneyA skilled team is not an asset line
Accounting periodResults are measured per periodIndian FY: 1 Apr 2026 → 31 Mar 2027
Historical costAssets recorded at acquisition costLand bought at ₹50L stays at ₹50L

Four concepts that drive profit measurement

Accrual

Record income/expense when the economic activity happens, not when cash moves. A March ad campaign paid in April is a March expense (with a payable at 31 March).

Dual aspect

Every transaction has two effects, keeping the equation balanced. This is the basis of double-entry bookkeeping.

Matching

Pair each cost with the revenue it earned. Sell 600 of 1,000 shirts → expense only 600 units' cost; the other 400 sit in closing inventory.

Realisation

Recognise revenue when it is earned, not when cash arrives. A ₹12,000 annual Netflix plan is ₹1,000 revenue a month; the rest is "unearned revenue" (a liability).

Four guiding conventions

Consistency (same method period to period), Prudence (don't overstate assets/income under uncertainty), Full disclosure (give users the material facts), and Materiality (information matters if omitting it could change a decision).

✎ Try it — matching concept. FashionHub buys 1,000 shirts at ₹1,000 each and sells 600 at ₹1,800. What is the closing inventory value (₹)?

Only the 600 sold shirts become cost of goods sold. The unsold 400 × ₹1,000 = ₹4,00,000 stay on the balance sheet as closing inventory (an asset). Expensing the full ₹10L purchase would wrongly show a loss and hide ₹4L of stock.

5. Depreciation & amortisation

Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current (fixed) asset over the years it helps earn revenue — a direct application of the matching concept. Four terms you must know:

  • Cost — what the asset cost to acquire.
  • Residual (salvage) value — expected disposal value at the end of its life.
  • Useful life — the number of years of use.
  • Depreciable amount = Cost − Residual value.

Method 1 — Straight-Line (SLM)

Same charge every year
$$\text{Annual depreciation} = \dfrac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual value}}{\text{Useful life}}$$
Worked example Machine: cost ₹10,00,000, residual ₹1,00,000, life 5 years ›
Depreciable amount = 10,00,000 − 1,00,000 = ₹9,00,000.
Annual charge = 9,00,000 ÷ 5 = ₹1,80,000 every year.

ℹ️ Intangibles & amortisation Session 1

Intangible assets (patents, trademarks, software, brands) have no physical form but give future benefit. Their cost is spread out as amortisation (the same idea as depreciation). A ₹10-crore patent over a 10-year life = ₹1 crore/year; carrying value after 3 years = ₹7 crore. Goodwill is treated separately and arises only in mergers & acquisitions.

6. The income statement

Measures performance over a period: Revenue − Expenses = Profit. You walk down it in a fixed order, and each subtotal has a name you must recognise:

💡 Exam tip — know these by name

Gross profit = Revenue − COGS. EBIT / Operating profit = Gross profit − operating expenses. PBT = EBIT + other income − finance cost. PAT = PBT − tax. EPS = PAT ÷ number of shares. Questions love to ask for one subtotal given the others.

7. The balance sheet

A snapshot of position on one date: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Profit for the period flows into equity (as retained earnings), which is how the income statement and balance sheet connect. Depreciation is interesting: it lowers profit (and therefore equity) but is not a cash outflow.

8. The cash flow statement

The income statement shows profit; the cash flow statement explains the change in cash. It has three sections:

Operating

Day-to-day business — cash from customers, less cash to suppliers, employees, taxes.

Investing

Long-term assets — buying machinery/investments (out), selling them (in).

Financing

Capital — issuing shares/loans (in); buybacks, dividends, loan repayment (out).

Why profit ≠ cash

EventEffect on profitEffect on cash
Credit sale, not yet collectedUpNo cash yet
DepreciationDownNo cash out
Inventory bought, not yet soldNo effect yetCash out
Machine purchaseSpread as depreciationFull cash out now
Loan repayment / dividendNot an expenseCash out

A profitable business can still run out of cash. That's why we need all three statements.

Reconciling net income to operating cash flow (indirect method)

The rules
Operating cash flow = Net income + non-cash expenses − ↑receivables − ↑inventory + ↑payables
Worked example From PAT ₹67.5L to operating cash flow ›
Net income₹67.5L
Add: depreciation (non-cash)+ 20.0L
Less: increase in receivables− 30.0L
Less: increase in inventory− 15.0L
Add: increase in payables+ 10.0L
Cash from operating activities₹52.5L

Logic: add back non-cash charges (depreciation, amortisation). A rise in receivables or inventory ties up cash, so subtract it. A rise in payables keeps cash in, so add it. Decreases do the reverse.

Putting it together: Operating ₹52.5L − machinery ₹40L (investing) + ₹15L (financing: loan ₹20L − dividend ₹5L) = net change in cash ₹27.5L. Net income was ₹67.5L, yet cash rose only ₹27.5L — receivables, inventory and machinery absorbed the difference.

✎ Try it. Net income ₹40L, depreciation ₹12L, receivables fell ₹5L, inventory rose ₹8L, payables rose ₹3.5L. Operating cash flow (₹L)?

40 + 12 (add depreciation) + 5 (receivables fell → cash in) − 8 (inventory rose → cash out) + 3.5 (payables rose → cash in) = ₹52.5L. Remember a fall in receivables releases cash.

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity, true after every transaction.
  • Accrual, matching and realisation separate profit from cash.
  • Depreciation: SLM = equal charges; reducing balance = heavier early.
  • Income statement → profit; balance sheet → position; cash flow → movement of cash.
  • Profit flows into equity; depreciation cuts profit but not cash.
