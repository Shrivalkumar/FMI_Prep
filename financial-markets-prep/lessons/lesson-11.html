Derivatives — Special Products & the 2008 Crisis

Lesson 10 built the toolkit: forwards, futures, options and swaps. This lesson shows what happens when those tools are stacked on top of each other — packaging loans into securities, insuring loans with swaps, and selling "safe" notes to savers. The same products that made credit cheap also caused the worst financial crisis in living memory. The unifying lens is reliability engineering: ask always whether failures are independent or share a common cause.

In this lesson

1. Securitization & mortgage-backed securities

Session 16 A home loan is a wonderful asset for a bank — it pays interest for 20 years — but a terrible one too: the cash comes back painfully slowly, and the money is locked up the whole time. Securitization solves this by turning many slow, illiquid loans into securities that investors can buy and sell today.

The four-step flow

StepWhat happens
1. Banks lendBanks and housing-finance companies lend to thousands of borrowers.
2. Loans are pooledThe loans are sold to a separate legal entity — a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) — off the bank's books.
3. Tranches are issuedThe SPV cuts securities into senior, mezzanine and equity layers, ordered by who absorbs losses first.
4. Investors get EMIsBorrowers' monthly payments pass through to investors; the bank recovers cash to lend again.

Think of it as a one-way conveyor. Loans and their default risk move right — from borrowers to the lender, to the SPV, to investors. Cash moves left, then right again — investors' money funds the SPV's purchase, the lender gets cash to lend again, and borrowers' EMIs flow back through the servicer to investors.

🏗️ Engineering view — crumple zones Session 16

Tranching works like a car's crumple zones. In a crash, the designed-to-deform parts (the equity layer) take the damage first so the passengers (the senior layer) are protected last. The structure decides who gets hurt and in what order.

Benefits vs risks

Benefits

Liquidity: a 20-year loan becomes a security that can be sold in a day.
Capital recycling: the lender gets cash back and lends again, so the same capital funds many more homes.
Diversification: one investor holds a slice of thousands of borrowers, not a single one.
Choice: tranches let cautious and risk-seeking investors each buy the risk level they want.

Risks

Moral hazard: if the seller keeps nothing, it has less reason to check borrower quality.
Opacity: buyers cannot inspect thousands of loans, so they rely on ratings and models.
Correlation: diversification fails when one cause hits every loan at once.
Complexity: each extra layer makes the real risk harder to see.

💬 Discuss

Why would a lender who sells every loan onward care less about borrower quality? Because the default risk leaves with the loan — this is the broken feedback loop that returns, with a vengeance, in Section 4.

Tranching & subordination

A tranche is a slice of the pool's cash flows, defined by its place in a queue. Payments flow down in strict order — senior first, then mezzanine, then equity — so the senior layer is safest and earns least, and the equity layer earns the most when all goes well but dries up first when borrowers stop paying. This loss-absorption ordering is called subordination, and it is the main credit enhancement in a securitization.

Worked example The ₹500 crore pool — who gets paid? ›
Setup A pool of ₹500 crore of loans at 9% earns ₹45 crore of interest a year. Payments fill the tranches like a pipe network — the senior pipe first.
OrderTrancheSizePromised returnPaid a year
1stSenior₹400 cr7.5%₹30.0 cr
2ndMezzanine₹70 cr11%₹7.7 cr
3rdEquity₹30 crwhatever is left₹7.3 cr ≈ 24%
Working Senior takes 7.5% × 400 = ₹30 cr. Mezzanine takes 11% × 70 = ₹7.7 cr. That leaves 45 − 30 − 7.7 = ₹7.3 cr for the equity tranche, which is 7.3 ÷ 30 ≈ 24% on its ₹30 cr.
Equity earns ~24% — far above the 7.5% senior rate — precisely because it stands last in the payment queue and first in the loss queue.

Interpretation. If borrowers pay less, the shortfall hits from the bottom up. Collect only ₹40 cr → senior and mezzanine are paid in full (₹37.7 cr) and equity gets just ₹2.3 cr. Collect ₹32 cr → senior is paid, mezzanine is short ₹5.7 cr, and equity gets nothing. Losses run in reverse of payments: equity absorbs first, then mezzanine, then senior.

The loss waterfall

Pool 1,000 loans of about ₹60 lakh each into that same ₹500 crore structure. Here is who loses what as pool losses grow:

ScenarioPool lossEquity (₹30 cr)Mezzanine (₹70 cr)Senior (₹400 cr)
Normal defaults₹25 crLoses ₹25 crSafeSafe
Bad year₹60 crWiped outLoses ₹30 crSafe
Nationwide crash₹150 crWiped outWiped outLoses ₹50 cr (12.5%)

The equity ₹30 cr soaks up the first ₹30 cr of loss; mezzanine the next ₹70 cr (up to ₹100 cr cumulative); only beyond ₹100 cr does the senior tranche bleed. At a ₹150 cr pool loss, senior loses 150 − 100 = ₹50 cr, or 12.5% of its ₹400 cr.

Independent defaults vs common-mode failure

Independent defaults: each of 1,000 loans has, say, a 3% default chance unrelated to the others. Losses cluster near 3% of the pool — about ₹15 cr of ₹500 cr. The ₹30 cr equity tranche absorbs it; senior is untouched.

Common-mode failure: a nationwide price fall raises every borrower's default chance at once. Losses can reach 30% — ₹150 cr. Equity and mezzanine are wiped out and the senior tranche loses ₹50 cr. The exact same structure, a completely different outcome.

MBS in practice — US and India

US agency MBS — Ginnie Mae

A bank makes government-insured loans (FHA/VA), pools them, and issues pass-through securities guaranteed by Ginnie Mae (first such pass-through: 1970). The guarantee removes credit risk — investors are paid even if borrowers default.

But a new risk appears: prepayment risk. When rates fall, homeowners refinance and hand principal back early, forcing investors to reinvest at lower rates. The cash flow behaves like a tank whose outlet opens wider when conditions change.

India's PTCs

An NBFC that has lent ₹500 cr to truck owners sells the pool to a trust, which issues Pass-Through Certificates (PTCs) to banks, mutual funds and insurers. The NBFC keeps servicing the loans and passes collections through.

The lender's cash returns in months instead of years; investors get exposure to granular retail loans they could never originate themselves. Post-2008, RBI risk-retention rules require the originator to keep a share of the risk — so the seller still has skin in the game.

2. Credit spreads, CDS & CDOs

The credit spread

Session 16 A government bond is treated as (near) risk-free; a company must pay more to borrow. The gap between them is the credit spread, quoted in basis points (1 bp = 1/100 of 1%).

Credit spread
$$\text{Credit spread} = \text{company yield} - \text{government yield}$$

If a company borrows at 9% while the government pays 7%, the spread is 9% − 7% = 2% = 200 bp. A wider spread means the market fears default more. The spread is a live market sensor — it updates daily and carries a fear premium, not a slow forecast.

Rule of thumb — the spread prices risk
$$\text{spread} \approx \text{default probability} \times \text{loss given default (LGD)}$$

This is failure rate × consequence — the same logic as risk in safety engineering. Rearranged, it tells you the market-implied default chance:

$$\text{implied default probability} \approx \dfrac{\text{spread}}{\text{LGD}}$$
Observed spreadLoss if default (LGD)Implied yearly default chance
2% (200 bp)60% (40% recovered)2 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 3.3%
6% (600 bp)60%6 ÷ 0.6 = 10%
0.5% (50 bp)60%0.5 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 0.8%
✎ Try it — implied default. A bond trades at a 6% spread and if it defaults investors lose 60% (recovering 40%). What yearly default chance does the market imply (%)?

Implied default = spread ÷ LGD = 6% ÷ 0.60 = 10%. The spread is failure rate × consequence, so dividing the spread by the consequence (loss given default) backs out the failure rate the market is pricing.

Credit Default Swap (CDS) — a warranty on a loan

A CDS is insurance on a borrower. The protection buyer pays a fixed premium every period; the protection seller collects it and pays out if a credit event (failure to pay, bankruptcy, or in many contracts a restructuring) hits the reference entity. The reference entity is the company, country or security being insured — it is not a party to the contract and may not even know the contract exists.

PartyRole
Protection buyerPays the premium each period. Often a bank/fund worried about a borrower — or a trader betting on its decline.
Protection sellerCollects the premium; pays out on a credit event. Often a large insurer or bank.
Reference entityThe company/country/security insured. Not a party, may not know the CDS exists.

The annual premium is simply the spread (in bp) applied to the notional amount insured:

Worked example CDS premium at 200 bp, and a payout ›
Premium A bank buys protection on ₹100 crore of a company's bonds at 200 bp. Annual cost = 200 bp × ₹100 cr = 2% × 100 = ₹2 crore a year.
Stress The company weakens and the spread widens 200 bp → 600 bp. The same cover now costs ₹6 crore a year to a new buyer.
Default The company defaults; bondholders recover only 40%, a loss of 60% × ₹100 cr = ₹60 crore.
The seller pays the buyer the loss: ₹60 crore (cash settlement = face value − recovery), or takes the defaulted bond and pays face value.

Interpretation. The buyer paid ₹2 cr/year and collected ₹60 cr — provided the seller can actually pay. That caveat is the whole story of 2008.

✎ Try it — CDS annual cost. You insure ₹100 crore of bonds and the CDS spread is 600 bp. What is the annual premium (₹ crore)?

600 bp = 6%. Premium = 6% × ₹100 crore = ₹6 crore a year. Basis points are just hundredths of a percent, so always convert first: 600 ÷ 100 = 6%.

⚠️ Three features that surprised people

Naked CDS: a buyer needs no loss — and no bond — to collect. You can insure a house you do not own, so a CDS is also a pure bet on a company's decline.

Counterparty risk: the seller's ability to pay is not guaranteed. If the seller fails, the "insurance" is worthless exactly when you need it — the chain breaks.

Opacity: pre-2008 CDS trading was private and over-the-counter, so no one could see how much risk any single seller had taken on.

AIG, 2008

AIG's financial-products unit sold CDS protection on the "safest" top layers of mortgage CDOs, collecting steady premiums and holding almost no reserves. When house prices fell, it was downgraded and the contracts forced it to post cash collateral it did not have. On 16 September 2008 the US Fed lent it $85 billion for ~80% of the firm; total support exceeded $180 billion. A warranty company insuring one product line against the same flaw, with no reserve fund — a design with no safety factor.

Credit Suisse, 2023

After years of losses and scandals, its CDS spread already sat above peers'. When US regional banks failed in March 2023, its spread jumped within days as shares fell and depositors fled. UBS took it over on 19 March 2023. The spread worked as an early-warning sensor, and fear fed on itself — a positive feedback loop. Notably, some bondholders were wiped out in the rescue: even senior-sounding debt is only as safe as the structure around it.

CDOs — securities made from securities

The senior layers of an MBS sell easily; the middle BBB-rated tranches are harder to shift. The clever (and fatal) idea: pool many of those BBB middle tranches from different MBS into a new pool — a Collateralised Debt Obligation (CDO) — and tranche it again. Because the CDO's own senior layer is protected by the layers beneath it, a large share of it was rated AAA — even though every single piece inside was only BBB.

⚠️ Why the CDO AAA was a mirage

Hidden assumption: the BBB pieces would fail independently. If each had a 5% chance and failures were unrelated, losing more than a fifth of them at once is vanishingly unlikely — so the senior CDO layer looks AAA-safe.

Reality: every piece held loans exposed to the same national housing market, so they failed together (common-mode). The "safe" senior CDO layers lost large amounts. A second round of tranching did not remove the error — it multiplied it.

3. The 2008 financial crisis

Session 16 No single villain caused 2008. It was a systems failure — a chain of reasonable-looking links that, together, created a machine with positive feedback and no circuit breaker.

#Link in the chainWhat it did
1Cheap moneyLow rates raised housing demand; prices climbed and everyone expected them to keep climbing.
2Subprime teaser loansWeak borrowers got low "teaser" rates that reset higher after two years, assuming they could refinance.
3Originate-to-distributeLenders were paid per loan sold, so quality cost them nothing — broken feedback.
4MBS & CDOsLoans repackaged twice; ratings assumed independent failures, so many tranches got AAA.
5Leverage + thin CDS reservesBanks borrowed heavily to hold the paper; insurers sold protection with almost no reserves.
6Prices fallResets hit, defaults rose together, losses could not be traced. Lehman failed on 15 Sep 2008.
🔁 The feedback loop Session 16

Falling prices cause defaults → defaults force asset sales → forced sales push prices down further. This is positive feedback: a control loop that runs away from its set point instead of settling back to it. The only brake was central-bank liquidity acting as a circuit breaker.

One subprime loan, traced — the 2/28

A US family borrows $300,000 on a 2/28 adjustable loan: 6% for two years (~$1,800/month), then a reset. At an illustrative 9.5%, the payment jumps to ~$2,490 — up 38%. With prices falling they cannot refinance, so they default.

That one loan sat in a pool backing an MBS, whose BBB layer went into a CDO, whose AAA layer AIG insured. One default is tiny. Millions in the same pools broke the chain.

Lehman & the funding freeze

Lehman held mortgage assets funded mostly by short-term borrowing rolled over every few days. When lenders refused to roll it over, it had to dump assets into falling prices. A rescue collapsed over 13–14 Sep; it filed for bankruptcy on 15 Sep 2008 — the largest in US history, over $600 billion in assets.

The next day a money-market fund holding Lehman paper "broke the buck" (fell below $1/unit). Investors rushed to withdraw from similar funds, so short-term funding froze worldwide and even healthy firms struggled to pay routine bills. The Sensex fell from ~21,000 (Jan 2008) to ~8,000 by October.

Why Lehman froze funding: its collapse showed that supposedly safe short-term lending (money-market funds, repo) could lose money. Lenders everywhere stopped trusting everyone, so they stopped lending at all — a structure that stays up only while its support is continuously renewed suffers a run, which is itself a failure mode.

🔧 2008 as a failure analysis
  • Model risk — assuming independent failures → AAA on tranches built from correlated loans.
  • Hidden coupling — CDOs and CDS tied firms together invisibly.
  • No fault isolation — a cascade with no circuit breaker, so US subprime losses spread worldwide.
  • Broken feedback — originate-to-distribute removed the lender's cost of bad loans.
  • Thin safety factor — banks ran on high leverage and little equity.
ℹ️ What changed afterwards Session 16

Basel III raised bank capital and liquidity requirements; Dodd-Frank reshaped US regulation and pushed derivatives toward clearing; risk-retention rules force securitizers to keep skin in the game; and stress tests now check whether banks survive a severe downturn in advance.

4. Market-linked notes (MLDs)

Session 16 A market-linked note (in India, a market-linked debenture, MLD) is a debt instrument whose return depends on a market variable such as the Nifty 50. It is sold as "upside of the market, safety of a bond" — and it is genuinely two instruments bundled together: a zero-coupon bond (the principal protection) plus options (the upside).

Suppose you invest ₹10 lakh for 5 years. The issuer splits it:

≈ ₹7.13 lakh → the bond

A zero-coupon bond at 7% grows back to ₹10 lakh in 5 years (10 ÷ 1.07⁵ ≈ 7.13). This is the principal protection — it matures to your money back.

≈ ₹2.87 lakh → the options

The remainder buys call options that pay extra if the Nifty rises and expire worthless if it falls. This is your upside.

MLN payoff
$$\text{Maturity value} = \text{principal} \times \big(1 + \text{participation} \times \max(0,\ \text{index return})\big)$$

The participation rate is the share of the index gain you receive. It is below 100% for a simple reason: the options cost money, and the more you spend on principal protection, the less is left to buy upside.

Worked example ₹10 lakh, 5 years, 70% participation ›
Formula Maturity = principal × (1 + participation × max(0, index return)).
Working — Nifty up 50% = 10,00,000 × (1 + 0.70 × 0.50) = 10,00,000 × (1 + 0.35) = 10,00,000 × 1.35.
Nifty up 50% → note return 35% → ₹13.5 lakh. Nifty flat or down → max(0, return) = 0 → ₹10 lakh (principal back, if the issuer pays).

Interpretation. You capture 70% of the rise, not all of it — that is the price of the protection. And notice the sting in the tail: a plain 7% fixed deposit grows ₹10 lakh to ₹10,00,000 × 1.07⁵ ≈ ₹14.03 lakh, which beats the note even when the Nifty rises 50%. The note wins only in specific scenarios, and its "protection" assumes the issuer survives.

Nifty after 5 yearsNote return (70% participation)Value at maturity
Up 50%35%₹13.5 lakh
Flat0%₹10 lakh
Down 20%0% (principal protected)₹10 lakh

✎ Try it — MLN maturity value. Principal ₹10 lakh, 5-year note, 70% participation. The index rises 50%. What is the maturity value (₹ lakh)?

Value = 10 × (1 + 0.70 × 0.50) = 10 × 1.35 = ₹13.5 lakh. You receive 70% of the 50% gain = 35%, so the note pays ₹3.5 lakh above principal.

⚠️ Risks — the "protection" has fine print

Issuer credit risk: protection fails if the issuer fails. Lehman minibonds, sold to retail investors in Hong Kong and Singapore as safe income, ended up worth a fraction of face value when Lehman collapsed.

Opportunity cost: capped or partial upside can trail a plain fixed deposit (as the 7% FD example shows). Illiquidity: selling before maturity may be hard or at a poor price. Hidden fees: costs sit inside the structure, not on a bill. Complexity: payoff rules can hide how often you actually earn nothing.

✅ A buyer's five-question checklist
  • Who is the issuer, and how strong is its credit?
  • Can I draw the payoff for a rise, a flat market, and a fall?
  • What are the cap, participation rate and total fees?
  • Can I exit early, and at what price?
  • Would a simple alternative (an FD) give a similar result?
⚠️ Common mistake — the one idea the whole lesson turns on

Diversification protects against independent failures, not a shared (common-mode) cause. A thousand loans look diversified until one event — a nationwide price fall — hits them all at once.

A senior tranche is only as safe as the structure around it. AAA is a model output, not a law of nature; when the model's independence assumption breaks, the senior layer bleeds (recall the ₹50 cr senior loss, and Credit Suisse bondholders).

A note's "protection" fails if the issuer defaults. Principal protection is a promise by the issuer, not a vault — Lehman minibonds proved it.

🔑 Five takeaways
  • Securitization slices risk, but it only works if the loans fail independently.
  • A credit spread prices default risk (spread ≈ default prob × LGD); a CDS lets you insure or trade it.
  • 2008 was a systems failure: correlated defaults, bad incentives, leverage, opacity and flawed models.
  • A market-linked note is a safe bond plus a derivative — so the issuer's credit is everything.
  • Complexity hides risk. If you cannot draw the payoff and the failure modes, do not buy it.
