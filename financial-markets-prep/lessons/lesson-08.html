Valuation & Forecasting (FCF / DCF)

What is a company actually worth? Not its share price today — its real, underlying value, built from the cash it will generate in the future. This lesson teaches you to turn forecasts into a per-share value, step by step, using Free Cash Flow and the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model.

In this lesson

1. What valuation is

Valuation is the process of estimating the economic value of an asset, business or company. The core idea is simple and you have already met it in the Time Value of Money lesson:

The one idea behind all valuation
$$\text{Value of an asset} = \text{Present value of the future economic benefits it is expected to produce}$$

A business is worth the cash it will throw off over its life, brought back to today's rupees. Everything else — formulas, models, spreadsheets — is just machinery for estimating and discounting those future benefits. Sessions 11–12

Two ways to think about value

Intrinsic valuation

What are the company's own future cash flows worth today? You build value from the inside out — forecast cash flows, discount them. DCF is the classic intrinsic method.

Relative valuation

How much is the market paying for similar companies? You value by comparison — "peers trade at 20× earnings, so this one should too." This uses multiples such as P/E.

⚖️ Market Price ≠ Intrinsic Value

Market price is simply the price the asset is trading at right now — what buyers and sellers agree on today. Intrinsic value is an estimate based on the inherent strength of the asset, i.e. its ability to generate future cash flows. The two are often different, and that gap is exactly where investment decisions are made: buy when intrinsic value > market price, be cautious when it is below.

What valuation is used for

  • Investment decisions — is a share cheap or expensive versus what it is really worth?
  • Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A) — how much should an acquirer pay for a target?
  • IPO pricing — what price should new shares be offered at?
  • Sale or purchase of a business — agreeing a fair price between buyer and seller.
  • Strategic decisions — which division creates value, which destroys it.

Factors that drive the value of a business

FactorThe question it answersEffect on value
Cash flowsHow much cash can the business generate?More cash → higher value
GrowthHow quickly can those cash flows grow?Faster growth → higher value
RiskHow uncertain are the future cash flows?More risk → higher discount rate → lower value

ℹ️ A note on currency

Our worked examples use ₹ (rupees), matching the Indian company data in the slides. The lecture examples occasionally use $ — the method is identical whatever the currency, so don't let the symbol distract you.

2. Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Accounting profit measures performance; Free Cash Flow focuses on the cash actually available after the company has made the investments it needs to maintain and grow the business. It is the cash that could, in principle, be handed to the people who funded the business.

The master formula
$$\text{FCF} = \text{Operating Cash Flow} - \text{Capital Expenditure (CapEx)}$$

Why not just use profit? Because of accrual accounting, cash ≠ profit. A company can report a big profit while its cash is tied up in unsold stock or uncollected bills — or while it is pouring money into new machinery. FCF strips the accounting adjustments back out to reveal the real cash.

Worked example — ABC Manufacturing Ltd, FY2026

ABC reports the following for the year (figures in ₹ crore):

Particulars₹ crore
Net Profit after Tax1,000
Depreciation200
Increase in Inventory100
Increase in Trade Receivables50
Increase in Trade Payables40
Capital Expenditure — machinery300

At first glance it looks like the company earned ₹1,000 crore, so ₹1,000 crore is available to distribute. But net profit is an accounting measure, not cash. We adjust it line by line.

Worked example Building FCF from net profit — one adjustment at a time ›
Start Net Profit after Tax = ₹1,000 crore.
+ Depreciation ₹200 Depreciation was subtracted to get profit, but no cash left the business for it — it is a non-cash expense. Add it back: 1,000 + 200 = ₹1,200 crore.
− Increase in Inventory ₹100 The company bought ₹100 crore more stock than it sold. That cash is now sitting on the shelf. Increase in inventory = cash outflow: 1,200 − 100 = ₹1,100 crore.
− Increase in Receivables ₹50 Sales were made but ₹50 crore of cash has not yet been collected from customers — it is tied up with them. Increase in receivables = cash outflow: 1,100 − 50 = ₹1,050 crore.
+ Increase in Payables ₹40 The company received goods/services but has not yet paid suppliers ₹40 crore — it has effectively kept that cash. Increase in payables = cash inflow: 1,050 + 40 = ₹1,090 crore = Operating Cash Flow.

Net Profit1,000Starting point
Add: Depreciation+200Non-cash expense — add back
Less: Increase in Inventory−100Cash tied up in stock
Less: Increase in Receivables−50Cash not yet collected

− CapEx ₹300 The company actually paid ₹300 crore cash for new machinery. Unlike depreciation, CapEx is a real cash payment, so subtract it: 1,090 − 300 = FCF = ₹790 crore.
So ABC's "₹1,000 crore profit" converts to just ₹790 crore of free cash. Working-capital build-up and the machinery purchase absorbed the rest.
⚠️ Common mistake — signs of working-capital changes
  • An increase in receivables or inventory is a cash OUTflow (subtract it) — cash is tied up. A decrease releases cash (add it).
  • An increase in payables is a cash INflow (add it) — you are holding onto cash you owe. A decrease uses cash (subtract it).
  • CapEx is a real cash outflow; depreciation is not. Add depreciation back, then subtract CapEx. Don't double-count by forgetting one of them.
✎ Try it — compute FCF. A firm reports: Net profit ₹600, depreciation ₹150, inventory rose ₹80, receivables fell ₹30, payables rose ₹20, CapEx ₹250 (all ₹ crore). What is Free Cash Flow (₹ crore)?

OCF = 600 + 150 (add depreciation) − 80 (inventory rose → cash out) + 30 (receivables fell → cash in) + 20 (payables rose → cash in) = ₹720 crore.

FCF = OCF − CapEx = 720 − 250 = ₹470 crore. Watch the receivables line: a fall releases cash, so it is added.

3. FCFF vs FCFE

Free Cash Flow comes in two flavours, and the difference is simply whose cash it is.

Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)

The cash generated by the business that is available to ALL providers of capital — both equity shareholders and debt holders.

It is measured before payments to debt (i.e. before interest and loan repayments). Think of it as the whole pie of cash the operations produce, before it is split between lenders and owners.

Quick approximation
FCFF ≈ CFO − CapEx

Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)

The cash available specifically to equity shareholders, after meeting the cash-flow consequences of debt financing.

Start from FCFF, pay the lenders their interest, then add back any new money borrowed:

Quick approximation
FCFE ≈ CFO − CapEx − Interest + Net Borrowing
The link between the two
$$\text{FCFE} \approx \text{FCFF} - \text{Interest} + \text{Net Borrowing}$$

So FCFF is to the firm; FCFE is to equity. If you value using FCFF you discount at WACC (the blended cost of all capital) and arrive at Enterprise Value; if you value using FCFE you discount at the cost of equity (Kₑ) and arrive directly at Equity Value. Capital structure is exactly what sits between the two.

💡 Exam tip — match the cash flow to the discount rate

FCFF → discount at WACC → Enterprise Value. FCFE → discount at Kₑ (cost of equity) → Equity Value. Discounting FCFF at the cost of equity (or FCFE at WACC) is a classic error — the cash flow and the rate must belong to the same set of investors.

4. The DCF model

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) is the intrinsic valuation method. It puts the whole "value = present value of future benefits" idea to work in a repeatable recipe.

#StepWhat you do
1Forecast cash flowsProject FCF for an explicit high-growth period (say 5 years).
2Pick a discount rateWACC for FCFF; Kₑ for FCFE (see Lesson 7 for WACC and Lesson 6 for Kₑ).
3Discount the explicit flowsBring each year's forecast FCF back to today at the discount rate.
4Estimate terminal valueValue everything beyond the forecast with the Gordon growth formula, then discount it back.
5Sum to Enterprise ValueAdd the PV of explicit flows + PV of terminal value.
6Get Equity ValueSubtract net debt → Equity Value; ÷ shares outstanding → value per share.

Terminal value & the Gordon growth link

We cannot forecast forever, so after the explicit period we assume cash flows grow at a steady rate g indefinitely and collapse them into one number — the terminal value — using the growing-perpetuity (Gordon) formula:

Terminal value at the end of year n
$$TV_n = \dfrac{FCF_{n} \times (1+g)}{(\text{WACC} - g)} = \dfrac{FCF_{n+1}}{\text{WACC} - g}$$

This is the same mathematics as the Dividend Discount Model / Gordon Growth Model for a share, where next year's dividend grows forever:

Gordon Growth Model (DDM) — Session 12
$$P_0 = \dfrac{D_1}{K_e - g}, \qquad D_1 = D_0 \times (1+g)$$

For example, with a current dividend D₀ = ₹5, growth g = 6% and cost of equity Kₑ = 12%: D₁ = 5 × 1.06 = ₹5.30, so P₀ = 5.30 ÷ (0.12 − 0.06) = ₹88.33. Terminal value is just this same idea applied to free cash flow instead of dividends.

Worked example A small DCF terminal-value calculation ›
Setup Suppose the final explicit-year free cash flow (year 5) is FCF₅ = ₹790 crore, it then grows steadily at g = 5% forever, and WACC = 10%.
Next year's flow FCF₆ = 790 × (1 + 0.05) = ₹829.5 crore.
Terminal value at end of year 5 $TV_5 = \dfrac{829.5}{0.10 - 0.05} = \dfrac{829.5}{0.05} = $ ₹16,590 crore.
Discount it back to today This ₹16,590 crore sits at the end of year 5, so it must be discounted 5 years: $PV = \dfrac{16{,}590}{(1.10)^5} = \dfrac{16{,}590}{1.6105} \approx$ ₹10,301 crore.
Add this discounted terminal value to the PV of the five explicit-year flows to get Enterprise Value. Terminal value is usually the biggest single component — which is why the small gap between WACC and g matters so much.

Estimating the growth rate g — the retention method

Where does g come from? You can use historical averages or analyst projections, but a clean fundamental method is the retention growth model:

Retention (sustainable) growth
$$g = b \times r$$

where b = retention ratio = 1 − payout ratio (the share of earnings kept inside the business) and r = return on retained earnings (ROE). Intuitively: a company grows by reinvesting profits, so growth = how much it keeps × how well it reinvests.

Worked example Retention growth from EPS, payout and ROE ›
Given EPS = ₹20, payout ratio = 40%, return on retained earnings (ROE) = 15%.
Retention ratio b = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60 (the firm keeps 60% of earnings; EPS value just confirms the firm is profitable).
Growth g = b × r = 0.60 × 0.15 = 0.09 = 9%.
A firm that keeps 60% of its profit and earns 15% on it grows at 9% a year. This g can feed straight into the Gordon/terminal-value formula.
✎ Try it — retention growth. A company has a payout ratio of 30% and earns an ROE of 20% on retained profits. What is its sustainable growth rate g (answer as a %, e.g. 14 for 14%)?

b = 1 − payout = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70. g = b × r = 0.70 × 0.20 = 0.14 = 14%. Keep more, or earn more on what you keep, and growth rises.

From Enterprise Value to value per share

$$\text{Enterprise Value} = \sum \frac{FCF_t}{(1+\text{WACC})^t} + \frac{TV_n}{(1+\text{WACC})^n}$$
$$\text{Equity Value} = \text{Enterprise Value} - \text{Net Debt}, \qquad \text{Value per share} = \frac{\text{Equity Value}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}$$

Enterprise Value is the value of the whole firm (to all capital providers). Net debt (debt − cash) belongs to the lenders, so subtracting it leaves Equity Value — what belongs to shareholders. Divide by shares to get the per-share intrinsic value you can compare with the market price.

⚠️ Common mistake — terminal value must be discounted back

The terminal value is computed as at the end of the explicit period (e.g. end of year 5). It is a future amount, so it must be discounted back to today before adding it to Enterprise Value. Forgetting to discount it hugely overstates the valuation. Also don't confuse Enterprise Value (whole firm) with Equity Value (after subtracting net debt).

5. Multiples / relative valuation

Instead of forecasting every cash flow, relative valuation asks: what is the market paying for comparable companies? You take a value driver (earnings, EBITDA, sales) and multiply it by a market multiple from peers.

MultipleFormulaValues…
P/E (Price-to-Earnings)Share price ÷ EPSEquity — "how many rupees per rupee of earnings"
EV / EBITDAEnterprise Value ÷ EBITDAThe whole firm, independent of capital structure
EV / SalesEnterprise Value ÷ RevenueFirms with little or no profit yet

P/E is an equity multiple; Enterprise-Value multiples value the firm before the debt/equity split (useful when comparing companies with different leverage). Peers are usually priced on a blend of intrinsic and relative methods.

🔑 Over- or undervalued?

Compare your DCF per-share value with the market price. If the discounted (intrinsic) per-share value is less than the market price → the share may be overvalued (the market is paying more than the cash flows justify). If intrinsic value is above the market price, it may be undervalued — a potential buy.

6. Summary

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Value = present value of future economic benefits. Intrinsic (build from cash flows) vs relative (compare to peers). Market price ≠ intrinsic value.
  • FCF = Operating Cash Flow − CapEx. Add back depreciation; adjust working capital (↑ inventory/receivables = cash out, ↑ payables = cash in); subtract CapEx. ABC: 1,000 → OCF 1,090 → FCF ₹790 crore.
  • FCFF = cash to all capital providers (before debt) → discount at WACC → Enterprise Value. FCFE ≈ CFO − CapEx − interest + net borrowing → discount at Kₑ → Equity Value.
  • DCF: forecast FCF, discount at WACC, add terminal value (Gordon: $FCF_{n+1}/(WACC-g)$, then discount back), sum → Enterprise Value, − net debt → Equity Value, ÷ shares → per share.
  • g = b × r (retention × ROE). DDM link: $P_0 = D_1/(K_e - g)$.
  • Multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA) give a quick peer-based value. If intrinsic value < market price → possibly overvalued.
