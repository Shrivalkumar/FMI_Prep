Derivatives — Foundations

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else — a stock, an index, a barrel of crude, a currency, an interest rate. This lesson builds the idea from zero: what a derivative is, why airlines and banks use them, the four main types, how call and put payoffs work, and a gentle look at the Black-Scholes pricing model.

In this lesson

1. What is a derivative?

A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived from an underlying asset. You are not trading the asset itself — you are trading a contract that refers to it. Sessions 14–15

The clearest way to feel this is with two everyday analogies:

🥛 Milk → Butter

Butter, cheese and ghee are derived from milk. Their price moves with the price of milk. In the same way, a derivative contract is "made from" an underlying asset and its value tracks that asset.

🛢️ Crude → Petrol

Petrol and diesel are derived from crude oil — when crude gets costlier, so do they. A financial derivative behaves the same way: its value rises and falls with the underlying it is built on.

The underlying asset is simply the thing the contract is based on. It can be many things:

  • Stocks — e.g. Tata Motors, Reliance.
  • Indices — e.g. the Nifty 50 or the Sensex.
  • Commodities — crude oil, gold, wheat.
  • Currencies — USD/INR and other exchange rates.
  • Interest rates — e.g. a benchmark lending rate.
The one-line definition

A derivative has no value of its own. Its price is entirely a function of the underlying. If the underlying vanished, so would the contract's meaning.

2. Why use derivatives?

Derivatives exist for two big reasons. The exam loves asking you to tell them apart. Sessions 14–15

① Speculation

Trying to profit from expected price movements without needing to own the underlying asset. A trader who believes Nifty will rise can take a position that gains if it does — using far less money than buying the whole basket of shares.

② Risk management (hedging)

Using a derivative to reduce or offset a risk you already face. You lock in a future price today so a bad move later cannot hurt you.

Two real-world hedging cases from the sessions:

  • Airlines hedge fuel. Jet fuel is a huge cost. An airline can lock in a fuel price now so that if crude spikes next quarter, its costs are already fixed.
  • Banks hedge interest rates. A bank with floating-rate exposure can use derivatives to protect itself if rates move against it.
💡 Exam tip

Same tool, opposite intent. A speculator takes on risk hoping to profit; a hedger gives up some upside to remove a risk. Whether a trade is "hedging" or "speculation" depends on whether the person already has the underlying exposure.

Every derivative contract is described by a standard set of features. Learn the vocabulary — questions quote these terms directly. Sessions 14–15

4. The four types of derivative

There are four families. The sessions focus on how they differ in obligation, standardization and what gets exchanged. Sessions 14–15

Futures

A standardized, exchange-traded contract to buy or sell an asset at an agreed price on a specified future date. Both sides are obligated to go through with it. Because the exchange sets the terms and guarantees settlement, futures are liquid and transparent.

Forwards

A customized, OTC contract between two parties to buy or sell an asset at an agreed price on a future date. Economically it is "a private future" — the two parties set the quantity, quality and date to suit themselves. Both sides are obligated. The trade-off is higher counterparty risk and lower transparency.

FuturesForwards
Where it tradesExchangeOver-the-counter (private)
TermsStandardizedCustomized
Counterparty riskLow (clearing house)Higher

Options

A contract that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying at the strike price on or before expiry. For this right the buyer pays a premium. This "right not obligation" is the defining feature of options — and it is what separates them from futures and forwards. We explore payoffs in detail in the next section.

Swaps

A contract in which two parties agree to exchange cash flows (or liabilities) over time. The most common is the interest-rate swap.

🔁 Interest-rate swap — fixed ↔ floating

Company A has a floating-rate loan but wants certainty, so it prefers to pay a fixed rate. Company B has a fixed-rate loan but believes rates will fall, so it prefers floating. They swap: A pays B a fixed rate; B pays A a floating rate. Each ends up with the exposure it wanted, without re-doing its original loan. That exchange of fixed for floating cash flows is the swap. Added for depth

The four at a glance
  • Futures — standardized exchange contract; both sides obligated.
  • Forwards — customized OTC contract; both sides obligated.
  • Options — a right, not an obligation, for the buyer (who pays a premium).
  • Swaps — an exchange of cash flows, e.g. fixed ↔ floating interest.

5. Option payoffs — calls and puts

Options come in two flavours. Getting these right is the heart of the lesson. Sessions 14–15

📈 Call option — the right to BUY

A call gives you the right to buy the underlying at the strike price. You want it when you expect the price to rise — you can buy cheap (at strike) and the asset is worth more.

📉 Put option — the right to SELL

A put gives you the right to sell the underlying at the strike price. You want it when you expect the price to fall — you can sell high (at strike) even though the market has dropped.

The payoff formulas

At expiry, with spot price $S$ and strike $X$, the option's intrinsic value is:

Call payoff (right to buy)
$$\text{Call payoff} = \max(0,\; S - X)$$
Put payoff (right to sell)
$$\text{Put payoff} = \max(0,\; X - S)$$

The $\max(0, \cdot)$ captures "right not obligation": if exercising would lose money, the buyer simply walks away and the payoff is zero. The buyer's net profit also subtracts the premium paid:

$$\text{Net profit (call buyer)} = \max(0,\; S - X) - \text{premium}$$

🛡️ The ₹1000 stock + ₹800 put example

You own a stock trading at ₹1000 and you are worried it might crash. You buy a put with a ₹800 strike. Now, no matter how far the price falls, you can still sell at ₹800. The put acts like a floor — it caps your loss. If the price instead rises, you let the put expire and keep all the upside (losing only the premium). That is hedging in one picture.

A text payoff diagram — long call

Picture profit on the vertical axis and the spot price on the horizontal axis. Below the strike the line is flat (you lose only the premium); past the strike it slopes up at 45°; it crosses zero at the break-even = strike + premium.

✎ Try it — call net payoff. You buy a call with strike ₹1,000 for a premium of ₹50. At expiry the spot is ₹1,100. What is your net payoff (₹) = max(0, Spot − Strike) − premium?

Intrinsic value = max(0, 1,100 − 1,000) = ₹100. Subtract the ₹50 premium → net payoff = ₹50. (Break-even was ₹1,050, and ₹1,100 is ₹50 above it.)

⚠️ Common mistake

Three traps students fall into:

  • An option buyer's loss is limited to the premium — it can never be more, however badly the trade goes.
  • An option seller's loss can be very large (potentially unlimited), while the seller's gain is only the premium.
  • Futures obligate both sides; options do not. The option buyer can walk away. Saying "a call forces you to buy" is wrong — it only gives the right.

6. Hedging in real life

Derivatives feel abstract until you see them protecting ordinary people and companies. Three examples from the sessions (and two familiar extensions). Sessions 14–15

Farmer & mill — a forward at ₹25,000/ton

A farmer growing wheat fears the price will fall by harvest. A flour mill that buys wheat fears the price will rise. They sign a forward contract to trade at a fixed ₹25,000 per ton on a future date. Now:

  • If the market price falls, the farmer is protected — he still gets ₹25,000.
  • If the market price rises, the mill is protected — it still pays only ₹25,000.

Both have given up a possible windfall in exchange for certainty. That is hedging — and a perfect example of a customized OTC forward.

👔 ESOPs behave like call options

Employee stock option plans give staff the right to buy company shares at a fixed price in the future. If the share price rises above that price, employees exercise and profit; if not, they simply don't. Right, not obligation — exactly a call option. Added for depth

🚗 Car insurance behaves like a put

You pay a premium; if your car is damaged (its "value falls"), you can claim and recover a fixed amount. If nothing happens, you simply lose the premium. A protective floor against a fall in value — exactly a put option. Added for depth

💡 Exam tip — the "right not obligation" analogy

The sessions compare exercising an option to deciding whether to attend a class: you have the right to go but no obligation. If it is useful, you attend (exercise); if not, you skip (let it lapse). That captures the buyer's freedom perfectly.

7. Black-Scholes — a pricing primer

So far we have found an option's value at expiry. But how much should the premium be today, before expiry? The Black-Scholes model gives a theoretical fair price. You do not need to compute it by hand — just understand its inputs and intuition. Sessions 14–15 Added for depth

The five inputs

InputSymbolEffect on a call's price
Underlying (spot) price$S_0$Higher spot → higher call value
Exercise (strike) price$X$Higher strike → lower call value
Time to expiry$t$More time → higher value (more can happen)
Risk-free rate$r$Higher rate → higher call value
Volatility$\sigma$Higher volatility → higher option price
The key intuition — volatility

The single most important idea: higher volatility raises an option's price. A more volatile underlying is more likely to make a big favourable move — and because the buyer's downside is capped at the premium, that extra "upside chance" is worth paying for.

The formula (for reference only)

Shown so you recognise it — no hand calculation is expected:

Call price
$$C = S_0\,N(d_1) - X\,e^{-rt}\,N(d_2)$$
Put price
$$P = X\,e^{-rt}\,N(-d_2) - S_0\,N(-d_1)$$

Here $N(\cdot)$ is the cumulative normal distribution and $d_1, d_2$ bundle together the five inputs above. The term $X\,e^{-rt}$ is simply the strike discounted back to today using the risk-free rate.

ℹ️ What to take away

Black-Scholes gives a theoretical price, not a guaranteed one. It assumes returns are normally distributed (among other assumptions). Treat it as conceptual: know the inputs, know that volatility drives price up, and know that the formula discounts the strike at the risk-free rate.

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • A derivative's value is derived from an underlying (stock, index, commodity, currency, rate).
  • Two uses: speculation (profit from moves) and hedging (remove a risk).
  • Futures = standardized/exchange; Forwards = customized/OTC; both obligate. Options = right not obligation; Swaps = exchange of cash flows (fixed ↔ floating).
  • Call = right to buy (profits when price rises); Put = right to sell (profits when price falls). Payoff = max(0, S−X) / max(0, X−S).
  • Buyer: loss capped at premium, profit ~ unlimited. Seller: profit capped at premium, loss potentially unlimited.
  • Black-Scholes: five inputs; higher volatility → higher price; theoretical, assumes normal returns.
