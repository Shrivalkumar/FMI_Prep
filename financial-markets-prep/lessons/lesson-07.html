Lesson 7 Session 10 · Bonds, Stocks & WACC

Bonds, Stock Valuation & WACC

A firm raises money from two broad camps: lenders (debt) and owners (equity). This lesson shows how to put a price on each — pricing bonds from their cash flows, valuing shares with dividend models — and then blends the two into the single blended rate every analyst lives by: the Weighted Average Cost of Capital.

In this lesson

1. Features of debt & bond terminology

Before pricing anything, understand why debt behaves differently from equity. When a company borrows — through a term loan or by issuing bonds — it takes on a legally binding promise. Six features define debt as a source of finance Session 10:

FeatureWhat it means
Contractual obligationThe issuer must pay interest and repay principal regardless of profit. Miss it and you default.
Fixed periodic paymentsThe coupon is pre-set — unlike equity dividends, which are discretionary.
Priority over equityDebt holders are paid before shareholders, even on liquidation.
No ownership / votingBondholders are creditors, not owners — no vote in how the company is run.
Finite maturity (usually)Most debt has a fixed repayment date. Perpetual bonds are the exception.
Tax-deductible interestInterest lowers taxable income — the tax shield on debt. (Dividends get no such shield.)
ℹ️ Real-world illustration Session 10

When Reliance Industries issued ₹20,000 crore of secured NCDs (face value ₹1,00,000 each) in Nov 2023, it took on a fixed obligation to pay interest and repay the principal by 2033 — with no ownership stake handed to investors. That is debt in one sentence.

What a bond is

A bond is a debt security issued by a company, bank, PSU or the Government of India to raise long-term funds. The issuer promises a fixed periodic coupon (interest) and repayment of the face value (principal) on a set maturity date. Think of it as an IOU: you lend ₹1,000 today, it pays you fixed interest, and returns your ₹1,000 at maturity. Issuers range from the Government (G-Secs, T-Bills) to PSUs (NHAI, REC, PFC, IRFC), banks (SBI, HDFC Bank AT1/Tier-2) and corporates (Reliance, Tata Steel, M&M NCDs).

Key bond terminology

TermSymbolMeaning
Nominal / Face value$F$Value printed on the bond (₹100 / ₹1,000 / ₹1,00,000) — also the amount repaid at redemption.
Coupon rate—Stated annual interest rate applied to face value, e.g. 8% on ₹1,000.
Coupon$C$Actual rupee interest paid each period. $C = \text{coupon rate} \times F$ (e.g. 8% × ₹1,000 = ₹80).
Redemption / Maturity value—Amount paid at maturity — usually equal to face value (at par).
Maturity date$T$The date redemption occurs and the bond ceases to exist.
Market price$P$Current traded price — may sit at a discount, premium or par to face value.
Yield to maturityYTMTotal return earned if bought at market price and held to redemption.
The coupon vs the coupon rate

The coupon rate is a percentage fixed forever on the face value. The coupon $C$ is the rupee cheque it produces each year. The required yield $r$ (what the market demands today) is a separate number — and the gap between the coupon rate and $r$ is exactly what makes a bond trade above or below face value.

2. Pricing bonds

A bond is worth the present value of every rupee it will pay you, discounted at the required yield $r$. All three bond types below are just this one idea with different cash-flow shapes.

Coupon (plain-vanilla) bond

A plain-vanilla bond pays a fixed coupon $C$ each year and returns the full face value $F$ at maturity $T$. Discount each coupon, then add the discounted face value:

Coupon bond price
$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{C}{(1+r)^{t}} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^{T}}$$
$P$
bond price today (what you pay)
$C$
annual coupon = coupon rate × $F$
$F$
face / redemption value
$r$
required yield per year
$T$
years to maturity

Interpretation: coupon rate 10% > required yield 8%, so the bond sells at a premium. You pay more than ₹1,000 today because the coupon is richer than the market demands — but redemption is still only ₹1,000.

Zero-coupon bond

A zero-coupon bond pays no periodic interest. You buy it at a deep discount and your entire return is the gap between purchase price and face value. With $C=0$, the coupon sum vanishes and only the discounted face value remains:

Zero-coupon bond price
$$P = \frac{F}{(1+r)^{T}}$$
$F$
redemption (face) value
$r$
required yield per year
$T$
years to maturity
Worked example NHAI zero-coupon bond ›
Formula $P = \dfrac{F}{(1+r)^{T}}$
Given $F=$ ₹1,00,000 · $r=7\%$ · $T=5$ yrs.
Working $P = \dfrac{1{,}00{,}000}{(1.07)^5} = \dfrac{1{,}00{,}000}{1.4026}$
Price ≈ ₹71,299

Interpretation: you pay ₹71,299 today, receive no coupons, and collect the full ₹1,00,000 after 5 years — a gain of ≈ ₹28,701, with nothing to reinvest in between (so no reinvestment risk).

A second case: REC Ltd zero-coupon bond, $F=$ ₹10,000, $r=8\%$, $T=10$ yrs → $P = 10{,}000 / (1.08)^{10} = 10{,}000 / 2.1589 \approx$ ₹4,632. Perfect for goal-based saving: buy one cheap share of a future ₹10,000.

Perpetual bond

A perpetual bond never matures — the issuer pays the coupon forever and the face value is never redeemed. The price is just a perpetuity of the coupon:

Perpetual bond price
$$P = \frac{C}{r}$$
$C$
annual coupon paid forever
$r$
required yield

In India these are mostly Additional Tier-1 (AT1) bonds issued by banks (SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI, PNB) under Basel III. Example — Bank of Baroda AT1: $F=$ ₹100 (never redeemed), coupon 8% → $C=$ ₹8/yr, required yield $r=8.5\%$ → $P = 8 / 0.085 \approx$ ₹94.12. Note the face value plays no part in the formula at all — only the coupon and the yield matter.

ℹ️ A bond's safety is about where it sits Session 10

AT1 bonds rank just above equity and can even be written down under regulatory stress — as happened with Yes Bank's AT1 bonds in 2020 (~₹8,400 crore written off on RBI's direction). Being called a "bond" does not make it safe; its rank in the capital structure does.

🔑 The discount / premium rule
  • Coupon rate < required yield → price below face value → discount bond (Tata Steel).
  • Coupon rate = required yield → price equals face value → bond trades at par.
  • Coupon rate > required yield → price above face value → premium bond (M&M).

Try the calculator. Mount a coupon bond below — key in Tata Steel (F = 1000, coupon rate 8, r = 9, T = 3) and you should get ₹974.56. Set the coupon rate to 0 and it becomes a zero-coupon bond (try NHAI: F = 100000, r = 7, T = 5 → ₹71,299).

✎ Try it — price a zero-coupon bond. REC Ltd: face value ₹10,000, required yield 8% p.a., 10 years to maturity. What is the price today (₹)? Session 10

$P = \dfrac{F}{(1+r)^T} = \dfrac{10{,}000}{(1.08)^{10}} = \dfrac{10{,}000}{2.1589} \approx$ ₹4,632. No coupons — the whole return is the ₹10,000 − ₹4,632 ≈ ₹5,368 capital gain over 10 years.

3. Yield to maturity (YTM)

YTM is the total return you earn if you buy a bond at today's market price and hold it to redemption. It bundles the coupon income with any capital gain or loss versus face value into a single annual rate. The exact YTM is the $r$ that makes the pricing equation hold (an IRR), but a quick, exam-friendly approximation is:

Approximate YTM
$$\text{YTM} \approx \frac{C + \dfrac{F - P}{n}}{\dfrac{F + P}{2}}$$
$C$
annual coupon (₹)
$F$
face value
$P$
current market price
$n$
years to maturity

The numerator is the coupon plus the per-year capital gain/loss; the denominator is the average of face value and price — your typical invested amount.

Worked example Infosys-style corporate bond ›
Given $F=$ ₹1,000 · coupon 9% → $C=$ ₹90 · $n=2$ · market price $P=$ ₹980.
Working $\text{YTM} \approx \dfrac{90 + (1000-980)/2}{(1000+980)/2} = \dfrac{90+10}{990} = \dfrac{100}{990}$
YTM ≈ 10.10%

Interpretation: the stated coupon is 9%, but because you bought below face value you also pocket a ₹20 gain at redemption — lifting the true yield to ≈ 10.10%. Price below face → YTM > coupon rate.

Second case — L&T-style bond: $F=$ ₹1,000, coupon 7% → $C=$ ₹70, $n=3$, $P=$ ₹950. $\text{YTM} \approx [70 + (1000-950)/3] / [(1000+950)/2] = 86.67 / 975 \approx$ 8.89%. Buying at a ₹50 discount lifts the yield well above the 7% coupon.

🔑 Price vs face tells you YTM vs coupon

Price below face value → YTM > coupon rate (you gain on redemption). Price above face value → YTM < coupon rate (you lose a little on redemption). Price at face → YTM = coupon rate.

Try the calculator. Enter the Infosys bond (F = 1000, coupon 9, price 980, n = 2) and confirm ≈ 10.10%.

4. Stock valuation — the Dividend Discount Model

Valuing a share is the equity analogue of pricing a bond. A bond pays coupons then redeems the face value; a share pays dividends forever and has no redemption value. So we discount the dividend stream. Dividends now play the role coupons played — but they can grow.

Zero-growth model

If the dividend is expected to stay constant forever, the share is a perpetuity of dividends — exactly like a perpetual bond:

Zero-growth DDM
$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r}$$
$P_0$
share price today
$D_1$
next year's (constant) dividend
$r$
required return on equity (cost of equity)

Coal India: $D_1=$ ₹18 constant, $r=12\%$ → $P_0 = 18/0.12 =$ ₹150. NTPC: $D_1=$ ₹9, $r=10\%$ → $P_0 = 9/0.10 =$ ₹90. The model suits mature, high-payout firms that distribute almost all profit rather than reinvesting for growth.

Constant-growth (Gordon) model

Most companies grow dividends at a modest, sustainable rate $g$ forever. Discount a growing perpetuity, remembering that next year's dividend is this year's grown by $g$:

Gordon growth model
$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g}, \qquad D_1 = D_0(1+g)$$
$D_0$
dividend just paid (most recent)
$D_1$
next year's dividend = $D_0(1+g)$
$g$
constant dividend growth rate
$r$
required return (only valid while $r > g$)
Worked example HDFC Bank — constant growth ›
Given $D_0=$ ₹19 · $g=10\%$ · $r=14\%$.
Working $D_1 = 19 \times 1.10 = $ ₹20.90, so $P_0 = \dfrac{20.90}{0.14-0.10} = \dfrac{20.90}{0.04}$
Price = ₹522.50 per share

Interpretation: valid only because $r (14\%) > g (10\%)$. If $g \ge r$ the formula blows up — that is why it cannot value a company still in a high-growth phase.

Asian Paints: $D_0=$ ₹15, $g=8\%$, $r=12\%$ → $D_1 = 15 \times 1.08 =$ ₹16.20, $P_0 = 16.20/(0.12-0.08) =$ ₹405.

Two-stage growth model

Young Indian IT and consumer firms out-grow the economy for a few years, then settle to a steady rate. The two-stage model values them as the PV of the high-growth dividends plus the PV of the terminal (steady-state) price:

Two-stage growth
$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{D_0(1+g_1)^t}{(1+r)^t} \; + \; \frac{P_n}{(1+r)^n}, \qquad P_n = \frac{D_{n+1}}{r - g_2}$$
$g_1$
high growth rate for the first $n$ years
$g_2$
stable long-run growth rate thereafter
$P_n$
terminal price at end of year $n$ (Gordon value of stage 2)
$D_{n+1}$
first stage-2 dividend = $D_n(1+g_2)$
Worked example Two-stage: D₀ = ₹20, g₁ = 15% for 3 yrs, g₂ = 6%, r = 13% ›
Step 1 — forecast high-growth dividends $D_1 = 20\times1.15 = $ ₹23.00 · $D_2 = 23\times1.15 = $ ₹26.45 · $D_3 = 26.45\times1.15 = $ ₹30.42.

5. Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

A firm is funded by both lenders and owners, each demanding a different return. The WACC is the blended rate the firm must earn to satisfy all its capital providers — the minimum return a project must clear, and the opportunity cost of capital. It weights the after-tax cost of debt and the cost of equity by their share of total capital:

WACC — debt & ordinary equity
$$\text{WACC} = r_E\cdot\frac{E}{E+D} + r_D(1-T_c)\cdot\frac{D}{E+D}$$
$r_E$
cost of equity (e.g. from CAPM: $R_f + \beta(R_m-R_f)$)
$r_D$
pre-tax cost of debt — the bond's YTM, not its coupon rate
$T_c$
corporate tax rate
$E,\;D$
market value of equity and debt

The $(1-T_c)$ term is the tax shield: because interest is tax-deductible, each rupee of interest costs the firm only $r_D(1-T_c)$ after the tax saving. For a firm that also issues preferred equity, add a third weighted term:

WACC — with preferred equity
$$\text{WACC} = r_D(1-T_c)\cdot\frac{D}{E+P+D} + r_P\cdot\frac{P}{E+P+D} + r_E\cdot\frac{E}{E+P+D}$$
$r_P$
cost of preferred equity
$P$
market value of preferred equity (note: preferred dividends get no tax shield)

After-tax cost of debt

The cost of debt is the current rate the firm pays: the interest rate on a term loan, or the YTM on its bonds — never the historical coupon. With several sources, take a market-value weighted average. Then apply the shield: after-tax cost of debt $= r_D(1-T_c)$. For example, a 7.5% pre-tax rate at a 25% tax rate costs only $7.5\% \times 0.75 = 5.6\%$ after tax.

Book value vs market value weights

The weights $E/(E+D)$ and $D/(E+D)$ can be built from either book values (straight off the balance sheet) or market values (share price × shares outstanding for equity; book value of debt is usually a fair proxy for its market value). Managers often reach for book values because they are simple — but book equity reflects historical accounting, while market equity reflects investors' current expectations.

🔑 Why market weights are preferred

The ideal weights are the firm's target capital structure in market-value terms. For growing or asset-light firms, market equity can dwarf book equity, which changes the weights and therefore the WACC itself. Market values are the best available proxy for the future (target) structure.

Worked example Reliance Industries — market-value WACC ›
Cost of equity (CAPM) $r_E = R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f) = 7.0\% + 1.05 \times 6.0\% = 13.3\%$.
After-tax cost of debt $r_D(1-T_c) = 7.5\% \times (1-0.25) = 5.6\%$.
Market-value weights (₹ Crore) $E=$ 18,00,000, $D=$ 3,10,000, total 21,10,000. $w_E = 18{,}00{,}000/21{,}10{,}000 = 85.3\%$, $w_D = 3{,}10{,}000/21{,}10{,}000 = 14.7\%$.
Blend $\text{WACC} = 13.3\% \times 0.853 + 5.6\% \times 0.147 = 11.35\% + 0.82\%$.
Market-value WACC ≈ 12.2%

Interpretation: on book weights the same company gives only ≈ 11.1% (book equity ₹8,20,000 Cr, book debt ₹3,30,000 Cr → $w_E=71.3\%$, $w_D=28.7\%$: $13.3\%\times0.713 + 5.6\%\times0.287 = 9.48\% + 1.61\% = 11.1\%$). Because Reliance's market equity (₹18,00,000 Cr) far exceeds book equity (₹8,20,000 Cr), market weights load more onto the higher-cost equity — pushing market-value WACC above book-value WACC.

For a near-debt-free firm the gap is small. Infosys (β 0.75): $r_E = 7.0\% + 0.75\times6.0\% = 11.5\%$; after-tax debt $6.5\%\times0.75 = 4.9\%$. Market weights ($E=$ ₹6,20,000 Cr, $D=$ ₹8,000 Cr) give $w_E=98.7\%$ → WACC 11.4%; book weights ($E=$ ₹90,000 Cr, $D=$ ₹8,000 Cr) give $w_E=91.8\%$ → WACC 11.0%. Only a 0.4-point gap, because there is barely any debt to re-weight.

⚠️ Common mistake
  • Coupon rate ≠ YTM. The coupon is a fixed rupee cheque; the YTM is today's market return. Only when price = face value do they coincide.
  • Use YTM, not the coupon rate, as the cost of debt in WACC. The coupon reflects an old issue; the YTM reflects what debt costs the firm now.
  • Apply the tax shield only to debt. Interest is tax-deductible, so debt gets the $(1-T_c)$ term. Equity and preferred dividends are not deductible — never multiply $r_E$ (or $r_P$) by $(1-T_c)$.
  • Grow the dividend first. In the Gordon model the numerator is $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$, not $D_0$.
🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • A bond's price is the PV of its cash flows: coupon bond $P=\Sigma C/(1+r)^t + F/(1+r)^T$; zero-coupon $P=F/(1+r)^T$; perpetual $P=C/r$.
  • Coupon rate < yield → discount; coupon rate > yield → premium; equal → par.
  • $\text{YTM}\approx[C+(F-P)/n]/[(F+P)/2]$; price below face → YTM > coupon.
  • Shares are valued like bonds without redemption: zero-growth $P_0=D_1/r$; Gordon $P_0=D_1/(r-g)$; two-stage = PV(high-growth divs) + PV(terminal price).
  • $\text{WACC}=r_E\,E/(E+D)+r_D(1-T_c)\,D/(E+D)$; cost of debt = YTM; tax shield on debt only; prefer market-value weights.
