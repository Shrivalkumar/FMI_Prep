Lesson 9 Session 13 · Financial Modelling & Portfolio Theory

Portfolio Theory & Market Efficiency

Lessons 6–8 taught you how to value one company. But no sensible investor owns just one stock. This lesson zooms out to the portfolio: how combining many stocks tames risk, why some risk can never be diversified away, and whether anyone can reliably beat the market at all.

In this lesson

1. Financial modelling — a quick recap Session 13

Before portfolio theory, the class revisited where a stock's value comes from — the financial model you built in Lessons 7 and 8. A model is just a structured forecast of a company's future, built line by line and then discounted to today.

Line items — the building blocks

A model starts by projecting the key line items that drive value: sales (revenue), costs, the resulting profits, and ultimately the free cash flows the business throws off. Get these right and everything else follows; get them wrong and no amount of clever discounting will save you.

Line itemWhat it capturesWhy it matters for value
Sales / RevenueHow much the business sellsThe top line that drives everything below it
Costs & expensesCOGS, operating costs, depreciationDecide how much revenue survives as profit
Profits (EBIT, PAT)What's left after costs and taxBridge from operations to cash
Free cash flowCash truly available to investorsThe thing we actually discount

Discounting: near-term vs the perpetual horizon

A business is assumed to live forever, but you cannot forecast forever. So a model splits the future into two parts:

Near-term horizon (1–5 years)

Here you forecast each year's cash flow explicitly — year by year — because the next few years are the ones you can reason about with some confidence. Each year is discounted back to today at the WACC.

Perpetual horizon (beyond 5 years)

Everything after the explicit period is rolled up into a single terminal value using a perpetuity formula, then discounted back. This "beyond-year-5" chunk often makes up the majority of the total value.

The two-part shape of a DCF
$$\text{Value} = \underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{5}\dfrac{FCF_t}{(1+WACC)^t}}_{\text{explicit 1–5 yrs}} \;+\; \underbrace{\dfrac{TV}{(1+WACC)^5}}_{\text{perpetual horizon}}$$

Estimating Ke, Kd and WACC

The session stressed that real-life modelling is as much estimation as arithmetic. The discount rate itself has to be built from assumptions:

InputWhat it isHow it's estimated (recap)
Ke — cost of equityReturn shareholders demandCAPM: $K_e = R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$
Kd — cost of debtAfter-tax interest the firm paysYield on its borrowings × (1 − tax)
WACCBlended cost of all capitalWeight Ke and Kd by equity & debt share
ℹ️ Why this recap leads into portfolio theory Added for depth

Notice β (beta) sitting inside the cost of equity. Beta measures how much a stock moves with the overall market — and that single idea is the bridge from valuing one company to managing a whole portfolio of them. Everything in the rest of this lesson is really about risk: what it is, which parts you can remove, and which you are paid to bear.

2. Markowitz Portfolio Theory Session 13

Harry Markowitz won a Nobel Prize for a deceptively simple insight: don't look at stocks one at a time — look at how they behave together. His Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) says an investor should choose a combination of assets that gives the lowest possible risk for a target return (or the highest return for a risk you can stomach).

Diversification — the core idea

Diversification means spreading your money across many different stocks and sectors instead of betting it all on one. The old proverb says it best: don't put all your eggs in one basket. If one holding collapses, the others cushion the blow.

The one-line takeaway

Diversification lets you cut risk without necessarily cutting expected return. That is the closest thing to a free lunch in finance — which is exactly why Markowitz called it one.

Why diversification matters — two kinds of risk

Here is the most important distinction in the whole lesson. Total risk splits into two parts, and diversification only kills one of them.

Unsystematic (specific) riskSystematic (market) risk
What it isRisk unique to one company or sectorRisk that hits the whole market at once
ExamplesA factory fire, a CEO scandal, a product recall, a sector-specific regulationHigh oil prices, an economic recession, interest-rate shocks, a pandemic
Can diversification remove it?Yes — spread across stocks and it averages awayNo — it affects every stock, so there is nowhere to hide
Also calledSpecific / diversifiable / idiosyncratic riskMarket / non-diversifiable risk
⚠️ Common mistake

"If I hold enough stocks, all my risk disappears." Wrong. No matter how many Indian stocks you own, a full-blown recession or an oil-price spike will drag them all down together. Diversification removes company-specific risk only; the systematic / market risk never goes away — and that is the risk beta measures and that you are paid a premium to bear.

Correlation and hedging

Why does mixing stocks reduce risk? Because of correlation — how two stocks' returns move relative to each other. Correlation runs from −1 to +1:

  • +1 (perfect positive): the two move in lockstep. Combining them gives you no risk reduction.
  • 0 (uncorrelated): they move independently. Mixing already lowers risk.
  • −1 (perfect negative): when one zigs, the other zags. This gives the maximum risk reduction — one stock's loss is offset by the other's gain.
Hedging in one sentence

Negative correlation lowers portfolio risk. Deliberately holding assets that move opposite to each other is called hedging — you accept a slightly lower peak return in exchange for a much smoother, safer ride.

Added for depth A classic illustration: an umbrella shop and an ice-cream shop. Rain is bad for ice cream but great for umbrellas; sunshine does the reverse. Own both and your income is steady whatever the weather — the two cancel each other's bad days. In markets, think of an airline (hurt by high oil prices) paired with an oil producer (helped by them).

Portfolio risk is NOT a simple average

This surprises people. If Stock A has 20% risk and Stock B has 20% risk, a 50/50 portfolio does not automatically have 20% risk. Because of correlation, the combined risk is usually lower than the average of the parts.

Two-asset portfolio — expected return (this one IS a weighted average)
$$E(R_p) = w_A\,E(R_A) + w_B\,E(R_B)$$
Two-asset portfolio — risk (this one is NOT a simple average — correlation ρ sneaks in)
$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{\,w_A^2\sigma_A^2 + w_B^2\sigma_B^2 + 2\,w_A w_B\,\rho_{AB}\,\sigma_A\sigma_B\,}$$
ℹ️ Read the formula, don't memorise it

The whole secret lives in the last term, 2 wAwB ρAB σAσB. When correlation ρ is low or negative, that term shrinks or turns negative and pulls portfolio risk down. Return is a plain weighted average; risk is not. That gap is exactly where diversification's benefit comes from.

Worked example Two negatively-correlated stocks lower volatility ›

Illustrative numbers — chosen to show the mechanism clearly, not real market data. Two stocks, each with expected return 12% and risk (standard deviation) 20%. We hold them 50/50.

Expected return = 0.5 × 12% + 0.5 × 12% = 12% — unchanged, whatever the correlation.
Correlation ρPortfolio risk σpWhat happened
+1.0 (move together)20.0%No benefit — risk = the average
+0.517.3%Some smoothing
0.0 (independent)14.1%Clear reduction
−0.510.0%Strong reduction
−1.0 (perfect hedge)0.0%Risk fully cancels out!
Same 12% return in every row — but risk falls from 20% to 0% as correlation drops from +1 to −1. That is diversification in a single table. With ρ = −1 the two stocks perfectly offset each other, wiping out the volatility entirely.

Added for depth Check one row: at ρ = 0, σp = √(0.5²·0.2² + 0.5²·0.2² + 0) = √(0.01 + 0.01) = √0.02 ≈ 14.1%. The cross term vanished because ρ = 0.

High-beta vs low-beta stocks

Within the market risk you cannot diversify away, stocks still differ in how violently they react. That sensitivity is beta (β) — the same β from the CAPM recap above.

High-beta stock (β > 1)Low-beta stock (β < 1)
Moves vs marketAmplifies it — rises & falls moreDampens it — steadier than the market
In a boomBig gainsModest gains
In a slumpBig lossesSmaller losses
Typical examplesCyclicals — autos, metals, real estate, many tech namesDefensives — FMCG (HUL, Nestlé India), utilities, pharma
SuitsRisk-tolerant investors chasing higher returnsInvestors wanting stability

A thoughtful portfolio blends the two: some high-beta names for growth, some low-beta names for ballast, so the overall ride matches your appetite for risk.

The efficient frontier

Now put it all together. If you plot every possible portfolio on a chart of risk (x-axis) against expected return (y-axis), the best ones trace a curve along the top-left edge. That curve is the efficient frontier.

Definition — the efficient frontier

The set of portfolios offering the maximum return for each level of risk (equivalently, the minimum risk for each level of return). Any portfolio below the frontier is inefficient — you could get more return for the same risk, or the same return for less risk, so no rational investor would hold it.

Added for depth You cannot climb above the frontier — that would mean free extra return with no extra risk, which the market prices away. You pick the single point on the frontier that matches your personal tolerance for risk: cautious investors sit lower-left (low risk, low return), aggressive investors move upper-right (high risk, high return).

3. The Efficient Market Hypothesis Session 13

Portfolio theory assumes prices are "fair". The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) makes that explicit: it argues that stock prices already reflect available information, so it is very hard to consistently "beat the market". How much information is baked into prices defines three forms of the hypothesis.

FormPrices reflect…If true, you CANNOT profit from…
Weak formAll past price and volume dataStudying historical price charts (technical analysis)
Semi-strong formAll publicly available information (plus past prices)News, earnings reports, analyst research (fundamental analysis)
Strong formAll information — public AND private (insider)Even inside information — nothing gives an edge
ℹ️ The forms nest inside each other Added for depth

Each form contains the one before it. Strong includes all public info (semi-strong), which includes all past prices (weak). Most evidence supports the weak and semi-strong forms reasonably well; the strong form is widely doubted — which is precisely why insider trading is illegal and can be profitable. If the strong form truly held, insider knowledge would already be in the price and worth nothing.

Market anomalies — where EMH breaks down

If markets were perfectly efficient, prices would always equal fair value. Reality shows anomalies — moments when the market clearly misbehaves, driven by human psychology rather than cool calculation.

Bubbles

Prices race far above any sensible value as everyone piles in for fear of missing out — then crash. Think the dot-com bubble (2000) or the 2008 housing bubble. Pure EMH struggles to explain these.

Irrational behaviour

Herd mentality, panic selling, overconfidence and greed push prices away from fundamentals. This is the field of behavioural finance — a direct challenge to the "rational investor" assumption behind EMH.

The honest takeaway from the class: markets are mostly efficient most of the time, which makes beating them hard — but they are not perfectly efficient, and investor psychology leaves visible footprints.

✎ Try it — conceptual check. An analyst reads a company's just-published quarterly results and tries to trade on them for a profit. Which form of EMH, if it held, would make this effort pointless? (Type: weak, semi-strong, or strong.)

Semi-strong. Quarterly results are public information. If the semi-strong form holds, that news is instantly absorbed into the price the moment it is released, so there is no lingering profit left for the analyst to capture. Only private (inside) information could still give an edge — and even that is ruled out under the strong form.

4. Active vs passive investing Session 13

EMH has a very practical consequence. If beating the market is genuinely hard, why pay someone a fat fee to try? That is the heart of the active vs passive debate.

Active investingPassive investing
ApproachFund managers pick stocks, time the market, aim to beat an indexSimply track an index — buy the whole market
Typical vehicleActively managed mutual fundsIndex funds & ETFs (e.g. a Nifty 50 index fund)
FeesHigher — you pay for the manager's research & tradingLower — little to decide, so costs are tiny
Long-run performanceMost fail to beat the index after feesOften outperforms active over the long run, mainly thanks to lower costs
EffortHigh — constant research and decisionsLow — set it and hold
The counter-intuitive result

You might expect expensive, expert-run active funds to win. Yet over long periods, low-cost passive index funds frequently beat the majority of active funds. The reason is twofold: (1) markets are hard to beat consistently (EMH), and (2) active funds' higher fees are a guaranteed drag that compounds against them year after year. In an efficient-enough market, cost is the one thing you fully control.

💡 How this ties together

The more you believe in EMH, the more sense passive investing makes — if prices are already fair, paying to hunt for mispricings is wasted money. The less efficient you think the market is (remember those anomalies), the more room there could be for skilled active managers to add value. Your view on market efficiency literally decides your strategy.

5. Summary & what to remember

🔑 Recap — the whole lesson in eight lines
  • Models forecast line items (sales → costs → profit → cash), discount years 1–5 explicitly and the rest as a terminal value, using Ke, Kd and WACC.
  • Markowitz / MPT: choose combinations of assets for the lowest risk at a given return.
  • Diversification removes unsystematic (company-specific) risk but NOT systematic (market) risk.
  • Negative correlation lowers portfolio risk — the basis of hedging.
  • Portfolio risk is not a simple average of individual risks; correlation is what makes diversification work.
  • High-beta stocks amplify the market; low-beta stocks dampen it.
  • Efficient frontier = maximum return for each level of risk.
  • EMH: weak = past prices, semi-strong = all public info, strong = public + private; anomalies (bubbles, irrationality) show markets aren't perfect. Passive (low-fee index) investing often beats active over the long run.
