Time Value of Money

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow. That one sentence sits underneath loans, EMIs, SIPs, bonds, share prices and every investment decision in finance. This lesson builds the whole idea from zero — compounding forward, discounting back, and the standard formulas for streams of cash.

In this lesson

1. Why money has time value

Imagine someone offers you ₹10,000 today or ₹11,000 in one year. Which is better? It depends on what you can do with money in the meantime. If a safe bank deposit pays 10% a year, then ₹10,000 today grows to exactly ₹11,000 in a year — you'd be indifferent. But if the phone you want rises 20% from ₹10,000 to ₹12,000, even investing your ₹10,000 leaves you short. That is the time value of money at work.

Four forces make money today more valuable than the same amount later:

Present consumption

People simply prefer to enjoy goods and services now rather than wait. To make you postpone, you must be paid extra.

Investing

Money in hand can be put to work — a deposit, a business, shares — so it grows. Idle future money earns nothing until it arrives.

Inflation

Prices rise over time, so each future rupee buys less. ₹100 next year will not stretch as far as ₹100 today.

Opportunity cost

Every rupee locked up until later is a rupee you could have used elsewhere. The best alternative you give up is the cost of waiting.

Simple vs compound interest

Interest is the price of time. There are two ways it can grow:

  • Simple interest is earned only on the original amount (the principal). The rupee amount of interest is the same every year.
  • Compound interest is earned on the principal and on interest already added — "interest on interest". This is what nearly all of finance assumes.

Watch ₹100 grow at 10% a year both ways:

YearSimple interest valueCompound value
1₹110.00₹110.00
2₹120.00₹121.00
3₹130.00₹133.10
5₹150.00₹161.05

Over 5 years, ₹100 at 10% earns ₹50 simple but ₹61.05 compound. The gap widens every year because compound interest keeps piling interest on top of earlier interest. The longer the horizon and the higher the rate, the more dramatic the difference.

💡 Exam tip — the symbols Session 7 · Speed Run

PV = value today · FV = value at a future date · CF = a cash flow at a point in time · r = the interest, discount or required rate per period · n = number of periods · A (or PMT) = an equal periodic payment · g = constant growth rate of cash flows. Get these straight and every formula below reads itself.

2. Compounding & discounting a single cash flow

The whole of TVM rests on one relationship read in two directions. Compounding moves a value forward in time; discounting moves it backward.

Compounding — PV to FV (forward)
$$\text{FV} = \text{PV}\,(1+r)^{n}$$
Discounting — FV to PV (backward)
$$\text{PV} = \dfrac{\text{FV}}{(1+r)^{n}}$$
PV
present value — the amount today
FV
future value — the amount after n periods
r
interest / discount rate per period (as a decimal)
n
number of periods

The factor $(1+r)^{n}$ is the compounding factor; its reciprocal $1/(1+r)^{n}$ is the discount factor. They are mirror images: compounding ₹10,000 at 10% for 3 years gives ₹13,310, and discounting that ₹13,310 back at 10% for 3 years returns exactly ₹10,000.

Reading the timeline both ways
Year 0: ₹10,000  —×1.10→  Year 1: ₹11,000  —×1.10→  Year 2: ₹12,100  —×1.10→  Year 3: ₹13,310   (÷1.10 each step going back)

Experiment with the calculator below — change the amount, rate and years and watch the future and present values move together.

Worked example Patel Industries' replacement fund (8%) ›

Patel Industries will need ₹50,00,000 in 5 years to replace a plant. It can earn 8% a year. Three questions, one relationship.

Formula FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ for compounding; PV = FV ÷ (1+r)ⁿ for discounting.
Given r = 8% = 0.08, n = 5, so (1.08)⁵ = 1.469328.
Compounding ₹10,00,000 invested today → 10,00,000 × 1.469328 = ₹14,69,328.
Discounting lump sum needed today for ₹50L → 50,00,000 ÷ 1.469328 = ₹34,02,916.
The firm can fully fund the ₹50,00,000 plant with just ₹34,02,916 set aside today at 8%.

Interpretation: compounding and discounting are the same relationship read in opposite directions. (We'll see in the annuity section that equal yearly deposits of about ₹8,52,282 reach the same ₹50L goal.)

A higher rate means a lower present value

Discounting divides by $(1+r)^{n}$, so a bigger r makes the denominator bigger and the present value smaller. Future money is worth less when you could earn more by waiting.

Future amountRateWorkingPresent value
₹50,000 due in 5 years8%50,000 ÷ 1.08⁵₹34,029
₹9,000 due in 5 years16.5%9,000 ÷ 1.165⁵₹4,193.85

At 16.5% the ₹9,000 is worth less than half its face value today. Going the other way, a credit card at just 2% a month turns ₹100 into ₹100 × 1.02¹² = ₹126.82 in a year — an effective annual rate of 26.82%, far above the "24%" headline.

⚠️ Common mistake — rate and period must match

A "12% per year" rate is not 12% per month. For monthly compounding use the monthly rate (12% ÷ 12 = 1% a month) and count periods in months (n = years × 12). Mixing an annual rate with monthly periods — or rounding factors like 1.08⁵ to two decimals too early — throws answers off by hundreds or thousands of rupees. Keep at least four or five decimals in intermediate factors and round only the final rupee figure.

Try the compounding calculator to see interest-on-interest build up year by year:

✎ Try it — future value of a lump sum. You invest ₹2,00,000 today at 9% a year for 4 years. What is the future value (₹)?

FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ = 2,00,000 × (1.09)⁴. Now (1.09)⁴ = 1.411582, so FV = 2,00,000 × 1.411582 = ₹2,82,316. The ₹82,316 of growth is pure compound interest.

3. Uneven cash flows

Real projects rarely pay the same amount each year. The method is simple: discount each cash flow by its own year, then add them up.

Present value of a stream
$$\text{PV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\dfrac{CF_{t}}{(1+r)^{t}}$$
CFt
the cash flow received at the end of period t
r
discount rate per period
t
the period number (1, 2, 3, …)
Worked example A machine's cash flows at 10% ›
Year (t)Cash flowDiscount factor 1 ÷ 1.10ᵗPresent value
1₹5,000÷ 1.1000₹4,545
2₹6,000÷ 1.2100₹4,959
3₹7,000÷ 1.3310₹5,260
4₹8,000÷ 1.4641₹5,464
Total PV₹20,228
The stream of ₹5,000 → ₹8,000 over four years is worth ₹20,228 today at 10%.

Interpretation: later rupees count for less — the ₹8,000 in year 4 is worth only ₹5,464 now, while the ₹5,000 in year 1 keeps ₹4,545 of its value. Pay less than ₹20,228 for this machine and it adds value.

ℹ️ Same idea, a café outlet Session 7 · Speed Run

A new café outlet at 12% is expected to return ₹3,00,000, ₹4,00,000 and ₹5,00,000 in years 1–3. Discounting each: ₹2,67,857 + ₹3,18,878 + ₹3,55,890 = ₹9,42,625. Any price below ₹9,42,625 makes the outlet worthwhile. This "discount each flow, then sum" method is exactly how NPV works in the next lesson.

4. Annuities — equal cash flows for a fixed period

An annuity is a stream of equal payments for a fixed number of periods — EMIs, bond coupons, SIP instalments, lease rentals. Because every payment is the same, we don't have to discount them one by one; there's a shortcut formula.

Present value of an ordinary annuity
$$\text{PV} = A\cdot\dfrac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}$$
Future value of an ordinary annuity
$$\text{FV} = A\cdot\dfrac{(1+r)^{n}-1}{r}$$
A
the equal payment made each period (also written PMT)
r
interest rate per period
n
number of payments

The fraction in each formula is the annuity factor. The PV formula is used to price loans, EMIs and leases; the FV formula values savings plans and SIPs.

Ordinary annuity vs annuity due

Ordinary annuity

Payments at the end of each period — EMIs, bond coupons. This is the default for the formulas above.

Annuity due

Payments at the start of each period — house rent, insurance premiums. Each payment sits one period closer, so it is worth (1+r) times more: multiply the ordinary answer by (1+r).

Worked example SIP, lease, and an annuity due ›

(a) A ₹10,000-a-year plan for 5 years at 10% (ordinary).

PV = 10,000 × [1 − 1.10⁻⁵] ÷ 0.10 = 10,000 × 3.7908 = ₹37,908.
FV (SIP) = 10,000 × [1.10⁵ − 1] ÷ 0.10 = 10,000 × 6.1051 = ₹61,051.

(b) A lease of ₹2,00,000 a year for 4 years at 9%, paid at year-end.

PV = 2,00,000 × [1 − 1.09⁻⁴] ÷ 0.09 = 2,00,000 × 3.2397 = ₹6,47,944.
If paid at the start (annuity due) = 6,47,944 × 1.09 = ₹7,06,259.
Paying lease rentals in advance costs ₹58,315 more in present-value terms — the price of paying a year sooner.

EMI link: rearranging the PV formula gives the EMI on a loan: A = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1+r)⁻ⁿ]. Patel's ₹50L goal (section 2) can also be met by equal year-end deposits: A = 50,00,000 × 0.08 ÷ (1.08⁵ − 1) = ₹8,52,282 a year.

Use the annuity calculator to flip between PV and FV and between ordinary and due:

✎ Try it — present value of an annuity. A lease pays ₹15,000 a year for 6 years, discounted at 10%, with payments at year-end. What is its present value (₹)?

PV = A × [1 − (1+r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r. The factor at 10% for 6 years is [1 − 1.10⁻⁶] ÷ 0.10 = [1 − 0.564474] ÷ 0.10 = 4.35526. So PV = 15,000 × 4.35526 = ₹65,329. (If the rent were paid in advance, multiply by 1.10 → ₹71,862.)

5. Perpetuity — cash flows forever

A perpetuity is an annuity with no end date — equal payments continuing forever. Preference shares, some endowments and consols (never-maturing bonds) behave this way. When n goes to infinity, the annuity formula collapses to something beautifully simple:

Level perpetuity
$$\text{PV} = \dfrac{C}{r}$$
Growing perpetuity (needs r > g)
$$\text{PV} = \dfrac{C_{1}}{r-g}$$
C
the constant cash flow each period (level perpetuity)
C1
the next (year-1) cash flow (growing perpetuity)
r
required rate of return
g
constant growth rate of the cash flow; must be less than r
Why r > g is essential

If growth g met or beat the discount rate r, the denominator (r − g) would be zero or negative and the present value would blow up to infinity (or turn negative — a nonsense answer). A perpetuity can only be valued when payments are discounted faster than they grow.

Worked example Preference shares & endowments ›
TypeExampleWorkingPV
LevelPreference share pays ₹500 a year, r = 10%500 ÷ 0.10₹5,000
LevelEndowment pays ₹6,00,000 a year, r = 8%6,00,000 ÷ 0.08₹75,00,000
GrowingFirst payment ₹500, g = 4%, r = 10%500 ÷ (0.10 − 0.04)₹8,333
GrowingFirst payment ₹6,00,000, g = 5%, r = 10%6,00,000 ÷ (0.10 − 0.05)₹1,20,00,000
Growth adds enormous value: the same ₹6,00,000 is worth ₹75 lakh as a level perpetuity but ₹1.2 crore when it grows at 5%.

Interpretation: a level perpetuity just divides the cash flow by the rate. Adding even a small growth rate shrinks the denominator and multiplies the value — which is why the "r − g" term is the heart of the growing-perpetuity (and terminal-value) idea.

6. Terminal value

No one forecasts cash flows forever. Instead we project them for a few years, then capture everything beyond the forecast with a single terminal value — a growing perpetuity sitting at the end of the forecast. You compute it in two steps: value the perpetuity at year n, then discount that lump sum back to today.

Step 1 — terminal value at year n
$$TV_{n} = \dfrac{CF_{n}\,(1+g)}{r-g}$$
Step 2 — discount it back to today
$$\text{PV of }TV = \dfrac{TV_{n}}{(1+r)^{n}}$$
CFn
the cash flow in the final forecast year
g
the perpetual growth rate after year n
r
discount rate; n is the final forecast year

Note that $CF_{n}(1+g)$ is just next year's cash flow — the first payment of the perpetuity — so step 1 is the growing-perpetuity formula with $C_{1}=CF_{n}(1+g)$.

Worked example Terminal value, two cases ›

Case 1 — year-5 cash flow ₹2,00,000, g = 3%, r = 12%.

Step 1 TV₅ = 2,00,000 × 1.03 ÷ (0.12 − 0.03) = 2,06,000 ÷ 0.09 = ₹22,88,889.
Step 2 PV = 22,88,889 ÷ 1.12⁵ = 22,88,889 ÷ 1.762342 = ₹12,98,590.

Case 2 — year-5 cash flow ₹50,00,000, g = 4%, r = 11%.

Step 1 TV₅ = 50,00,000 × 1.04 ÷ (0.11 − 0.04) = 52,00,000 ÷ 0.07 = ₹7,42,85,714.
Step 2 PV = 7,42,85,714 ÷ 1.11⁵ = 7,42,85,714 ÷ 1.685058 = ₹4,40,84,956.
The terminal value is a huge share of a project's total worth — here ₹13L and ₹4.4 crore respectively, in today's money.

Interpretation: because the terminal value dominates a valuation, small changes in g or r swing the answer a lot. And never forget step 2 — the perpetuity is worth that much at year n, not today, so it must be discounted back.

7. The Rule of 72

A handy mental shortcut: to find roughly how many years money takes to double at a given compound rate, divide 72 by the rate (in percent).

Rule of 72
$$\text{Years to double} \approx \dfrac{72}{r\,(\%)}$$
RateWorking≈ Years to double
6%72 ÷ 612 years
8%72 ÷ 89 years
12%72 ÷ 126 years

At 8%, money roughly doubles in about 9 years. It's an approximation (it works best for rates between about 4% and 15%), but it's quick, needs no calculator, and is a favourite exam question.

Worked example Four quick questions at 8% ›
Question (r = 8%)Answer
₹1,00,000 invested for 5 years — FV₹1,46,933
Value today of ₹1,00,000 due in 5 years — PV₹68,058
PV of ₹10,000 a year for 5 years (annuity)₹39,927
FV of a ₹10,000 yearly SIP after 5 years₹58,666

Perpetuity & a choice: ₹10,000 forever at 8% is worth ₹10,000 ÷ 0.08 = ₹1,25,000 today. And ₹1,00,000 now vs ₹1,20,000 in 3 years? PV of ₹1.2L = 1,20,000 ÷ 1.08³ = ₹95,260 — less than ₹1,00,000, so take the money now.

8. Summary

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Money has time value because of present-consumption preference, investing, inflation and opportunity cost. Compound interest beats simple because interest earns interest.
  • Compound forward: FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ. Discount back: PV = FV ÷ (1+r)ⁿ. They are the same relationship reversed. A higher r means a lower PV.
  • Uneven stream: PV = Σ CFt ÷ (1+r)ᵗ — discount each flow by its own year and add.
  • Annuity (equal flows): PV = A·[1 − (1+r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r; FV = A·[(1+r)ⁿ − 1] ÷ r. An annuity due is worth (1+r)× an ordinary one.
  • Perpetuity: level PV = C ÷ r; growing PV = C₁ ÷ (r − g), valid only when r > g.
  • Terminal value: TVn = CFn(1+g) ÷ (r − g), then discount back by (1+r)ⁿ. It usually dominates a valuation.
  • Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ rate%. Always match the rate to the period and round only at the end.
