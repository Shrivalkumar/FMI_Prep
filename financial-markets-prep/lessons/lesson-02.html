Financial Ratio Analysis

A single number on a statement rarely means much on its own. Is ₹3,588 crore of profit good? It depends on the sales, the assets and the capital behind it. Ratios turn raw figures into relationships you can read, compare and judge. This lesson builds all five families of ratios from zero, then applies every one of them to a real company — Asian Paints, FY2025.

In this lesson

1. Why ratios exist

A financial ratio is one figure from the financial statements divided by another, so that the result describes a relationship rather than a bare amount. Ratios are derived entirely from statements you already met in Lesson 1 — the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement.

They are useful for one reason above all: comparison. A ratio lets you compare a company:

  • with itself over time — is the current ratio sliding year on year?
  • with competitors and the industry — is our net margin below the sector's?
  • with budget — did returns land where management planned?

What do we want the ratios to tell us? The sessions list seven objectives: assess profitability, evaluate liquidity, measure operational efficiency, assess solvency and risk, evaluate returns on capital, enable performance comparison, and support decisions. Those objectives map onto five families you will use for the rest of the lesson:

FamilyQuestion it answersHeadline ratios
LiquidityCan we pay short-term bills as they fall due?Current, quick, cash
Efficiency (activity)How hard are our assets & working capital working?Inventory / receivables / payables turnover & days
Solvency (leverage)Can we meet long-term debt, and how risky is the gearing?Debt-to-equity, interest coverage
Profitability & returnHow well do we turn sales & capital into profit?Margins, ROCE, ROA, ROE
Market (investor)How does the stock market value us?EPS, P/E, yield, market cap, EV
💡 Exam tip

Learn each ratio as a four-part fact: (1) what it measures, (2) the formula, (3) how to read a high vs low value, (4) a rule-of-thumb. Questions test all four — a conceptual question asks what it means, a numerical one asks you to plug in and compute.

2. Liquidity ratios Session 3

Liquidity ratios measure whether a business can meet its short-term obligations — the bills due within about a year — out of its short-term assets and cash-generating ability. The question they answer: "Does the business have enough short-term assets to pay its short-term liabilities?"

Short-term liabilities include trade payables, short-term borrowings, bank overdrafts, accrued expenses, wages and taxes payable, and the current portion of long-term debt. Short-term assets include cash and bank, marketable securities, trade receivables, inventory and other current assets.

Current ratio

The broadest liquidity measure: how many rupees of current assets back each rupee of current liabilities.

Current ratio
$$\text{Current ratio} = \dfrac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

How to read it: above 1 means current assets more than cover current liabilities. Rule of thumb: 1.5–2 is comfortable. Too low (<1) signals a cash squeeze; much too high can mean idle cash or overstocking. Example: current assets ₹60L ÷ current liabilities ₹40L = 1.5× — every ₹1 due is backed by ₹1.50 of short-term assets.

Quick ratio (acid-test)

Inventory can be slow to sell and may not convert to cash easily, so the quick ratio strips inventory out and tests the most liquid assets only.

Quick / acid-test ratio
$$\text{Quick ratio} = \dfrac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

How to read it: a stricter liquidity test. Rule of thumb: around 1 is healthy. Example: current assets ₹60L, inventory ₹20L, current liabilities ₹40L → (60 − 20) ÷ 40 = 1.0×. Note the current ratio was 1.5× but the quick ratio is only 1.0× — ₹20L of the cover was tied up in stock.

Cash ratio

The most conservative test: only cash, bank and highly liquid marketable securities — no receivables, no inventory.

Cash ratio
$$\text{Cash ratio} = \dfrac{\text{Cash and cash equivalents}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

How to read it: shows the share of short-term bills payable instantly from cash. Example: cash ₹8L ÷ current liabilities ₹40L = 0.2× — about 20 paise of every ₹1 due is sitting in cash. A low cash ratio is not automatically alarming for a firm that collects reliably and holds investments elsewhere.

⚠️ Common mistake

Don't confuse the quick ratio with the cash ratio. Quick assets = current assets minus inventory (it still includes receivables). The cash ratio drops receivables too, keeping only cash and equivalents. A firm can have a decent quick ratio and a tiny cash ratio at the same time.

✎ Try it — current ratio. A firm has current assets of ₹90L (of which inventory is ₹36L) and current liabilities of ₹60L. What is its current ratio (as a number, e.g. 1.5)?

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities = 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5×. (Inventory is a distractor — it only matters for the quick ratio, which here would be (90 − 36) ÷ 60 = 0.9×.)

3. Efficiency (activity) ratios Session 3

Efficiency ratios — also called activity or asset-management ratios — measure how effectively a business uses its assets and working capital to generate sales. The question: "How efficiently is the company using its resources to generate revenue?" A business ties money up in inventory, receivables and fixed assets; these ratios ask whether that money is working hard.

Inventory turnover & inventory days

Inventory turnover counts how many times a year the firm sells and replaces its stock. We use cost of goods sold (not sales) because inventory is carried at cost.

$$\text{Inventory turnover} = \dfrac{\text{Cost of goods sold}}{\text{Average inventory}} \qquad \text{Inventory days} = \dfrac{365}{\text{Inventory turnover}}$$

where average inventory = (opening + closing) ÷ 2. Inventory days (Days Inventory Outstanding) is the same idea read as "days stock sits before being sold". How to read it: higher turnover / fewer days = stock moves faster = less cash locked up. Example: COGS ₹140L ÷ average stock ₹20L = 7× → 365 ÷ 7 = 52 days.

Receivables turnover & collection days

Receivables turnover measures how many times a year the firm collects its debtors; the matching day-count is the average collection period (Days Sales Outstanding / debtor days) — how long customers take to pay.

$$\text{Receivables turnover} = \dfrac{\text{Net credit sales}}{\text{Average receivables}} \qquad \text{Collection days} = \dfrac{365}{\text{Receivables turnover}}$$

How to read it: fewer collection days = faster cash in = better. Example: sales ₹200L ÷ average debtors ₹25L = 8× → 365 ÷ 8 ≈ 46 days.

Payables turnover & payment days

Payables turnover measures how quickly the firm pays suppliers; the average payment period (Days Payable Outstanding) is the day-count.

$$\text{Payables turnover} = \dfrac{\text{Credit purchases (or COGS)}}{\text{Average payables}} \qquad \text{Payable days} = \dfrac{365}{\text{Payables turnover}}$$
ℹ️ When purchases aren't given

If credit purchases aren't disclosed, COGS is used instead in the payables (and sometimes inventory) turnover. Exam questions usually tell you which figure to use.

How to read it: more payable days means you hold onto cash longer — useful, but stretching suppliers too far risks the relationship. Example: COGS ₹140L ÷ average creditors ₹28L = 5× → 365 ÷ 5 = 73 days.

Working-capital (operating) cycle

The operating cycle ties the three together. It is the time between paying for inputs and finally collecting cash from customers — the money tied up in day-to-day operations.

Operating / working-capital cycle
$$\text{Operating cycle} = \text{Inventory days} + \text{Receivable days} - \text{Payable days}$$

How to read it: a shorter cycle means less cash is trapped. Example: 45 + 30 − 35 = 40 days of cash tied up. You shorten it by selling stock faster, collecting sooner, or (within terms) paying suppliers later.

4. Solvency (leverage) ratios Session 3

Where liquidity ratios look at the short term, solvency ratios look at long-term financial stability and capital structure. The question: "Does the company carry a sustainable level of debt, and can it meet its long-term interest and principal obligations?"

Debt-to-equity (D/E)

Compares borrowed funds with owners' funds — how much the firm leans on debt.

Debt-to-equity ratio
$$\text{D/E} = \dfrac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Equity}}$$

where Debt = total interest-bearing debt (or total liabilities, depending on the definition used) and Equity = share capital + reserves & surplus (retained earnings). How to read it: higher D/E = more leverage = more financial risk (and bigger fixed interest bills). Rule of thumb: below 1 is conservative; above 2 is aggressive. Example: debt ₹50L ÷ equity ₹100L = 0.5×.

Interest coverage

Asks how comfortably operating profit covers the interest bill.

Interest coverage ratio
$$\text{Interest coverage} = \dfrac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest expense}}$$

where EBIT = earnings before interest and tax (operating profit). How to read it: the higher the better — it measures the cushion before the firm struggles to pay interest. Rule of thumb: below 2× is a warning sign. Example: EBIT ₹24L ÷ interest ₹6L = 4× — profit covers interest four times over.

Pair them up

Read D/E and interest coverage together. High D/E and low coverage is the danger zone (lots of debt, thin ability to service it). Low D/E and high coverage is a lightly geared, low-risk balance sheet — exactly what you'll see for Asian Paints later.

5. Profitability & return ratios Session 4

Profitability & return ratios measure how well a company turns sales, assets and capital into profit. Crucially, they never judge profit alone — a firm can have high sales but thin margins, or high profit but poor returns on capital, or high returns only because of heavy leverage. They split into two groups: margin ratios (profit relative to revenue) and return ratios (profit relative to capital, assets or equity).

Margin ratios

$$\text{Gross margin} = \dfrac{\text{Sales} - \text{COGS}}{\text{Sales}} \qquad \text{Operating margin} = \dfrac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Sales}} \qquad \text{Net margin} = \dfrac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Sales}}$$
MarginMeasures profit after…Reads as
Gross…only direct costs (COGS)Profitability of core production/buying
Operating (EBIT)…operating expenses & depreciation, before interest & taxProfitability of the main business
Net (PAT)…all expenses incl. finance cost & taxWhat finally reaches shareholders

Example: sales ₹200L, COGS ₹140L, EBIT ₹24L, PAT ₹14L → gross margin (200 − 140) ÷ 200 = 30%; operating margin 24 ÷ 200 = 12%; net margin 14 ÷ 200 = 7%.

Return ratios

Margins tell you profit per rupee of sales; return ratios tell you profit per rupee of capital.

$$\text{ROCE} = \dfrac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital employed}} \qquad \text{ROA} = \dfrac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Total assets}} \qquad \text{ROE} = \dfrac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Shareholders' equity}}$$
  • ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) — operating profit earned on all long-term capital. Capital employed = Equity + Debt (equivalently, Total assets − Current liabilities).
  • ROA (Return on Assets) — how efficiently the whole asset base generates profit.
  • ROE (Return on Equity) — profit generated on the owners' money. Shareholders' equity = Share capital + Retained earnings.

Example: EBIT ₹24L, capital employed ₹150L → ROCE = 24 ÷ 150 = 16%; PAT ₹14L, equity ₹100L → ROE = 14 ÷ 100 = 14%; and asset turnover = Sales ÷ Total assets = 200 ÷ 190 = 1.05×.

ℹ️ ROI — the broad cousin

Return on Investment (ROI) is a broader, less standardised idea: gain ÷ cost of a particular investment. Example: ₹1,00,000 growing to ₹1,25,000 is an ROI of 25%. Unlike ROE/ROCE it has no single accounting formula.

DuPont decomposition of ROE

ROE is powerful but it hides how the return is earned. The DuPont analysis breaks it into three levers you can read separately:

DuPont three-step
$$\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\dfrac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{Net margin}} \times \underbrace{\dfrac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Total assets}}}_{\text{Asset turnover}} \times \underbrace{\dfrac{\text{Total assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{Equity multiplier}}$$

The sales and asset terms cancel, leaving Net profit ÷ Equity = ROE — but now you can see which lever drives it: margin (how profitable each sale is), asset turnover (how hard assets work), or the equity multiplier (how much leverage). Two firms can reach the same ROE very differently:

IllustrationNet marginAsset turnoverEquity multiplierROE
Jewellery retailer20%0.5×1.5×15%
Grocery chain3%4.0×1.25×15%

Same 15% ROE — but the jeweller wins on margin and the grocer on turnover. Always check which lever is doing the work before comparing firms.

6. Market (investor) ratios Session 4

Market ratios judge the company from the perspective of equity shareholders and the stock market. They answer: "How does the market value the company's earnings, assets and growth?" They pull in market data — share price, market cap, EPS, DPS, book value — that the accounting statements alone don't contain.

RatioFormulaTells you
EPS (earnings per share)Net profit ÷ no. of sharesProfit earned per share
P/E (price-earnings)Market price ÷ EPSPrice paid per ₹1 of earnings
DPS (dividend per share)Total dividend ÷ no. of sharesCash dividend per share
Payout ratioDPS ÷ EPS (or dividend ÷ profit)Share of earnings paid out
Dividend yieldDPS ÷ market priceIncome return on the share price
Market capPrice × shares outstandingTotal equity value
Enterprise value (EV)Market cap + Debt − CashValue of the whole business (M&A)
Price-to-book (P/B)Price ÷ book value per sharePrice vs accounting net worth

How to read them: a high P/E means investors pay a lot for each ₹1 of earnings — usually pricing in growth. Dividend yield is the cash income a share pays relative to its price. EV captures both equity and debt value minus cash, which is why it's the metric used in takeovers. P/B above 1 means the market values the firm above its book net worth (brand, earning power, growth).

Example: PAT ₹14L with 1 lakh shares → EPS = ₹14. At a price of ₹210, P/E = 210 ÷ 14 = 15×. With DPS ₹4 → payout = 4 ÷ 14 ≈ 29% and yield = 4 ÷ 210 ≈ 1.9%. Market cap = ₹210 × 1,00,000 = ₹2.1 crore.

✎ Try it — ROE. A company earns net profit of ₹3,588 crore on shareholders' equity of ₹18,988 crore. What is its Return on Equity, as a percentage (e.g. 12.5)?

ROE = Net profit ÷ Shareholders' equity = 3,588 ÷ 18,988 = 0.189 = 18.9%. Each ₹100 of owners' money earned about ₹18.90. (These are Asian Paints' FY2025 figures — see the worked example below.)

8. Summary & recap

Putting Asian Paints' whole scorecard in one place shows how the families combine into a verdict:

🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Liquidity: current (CA÷CL, aim 1.5–2), quick (ex-inventory, aim ~1), cash (cash only).
  • Efficiency: turnover = activity ÷ balance; days = 365 ÷ turnover; operating cycle = inventory + receivable − payable days.
  • Solvency: D/E = debt ÷ equity (higher = riskier); interest coverage = EBIT ÷ interest (below 2× is a warning).
  • Profitability: gross / operating / net margins; ROCE (EBIT ÷ capital employed), ROA, ROE; DuPont: ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier.
  • Market: EPS, P/E, DPS, payout, yield, market cap, EV = market cap + debt − cash, P/B.
  • Golden rule: never judge a company on one ratio — compare over time, with peers and with budget.
