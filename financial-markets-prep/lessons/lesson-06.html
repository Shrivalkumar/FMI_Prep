Lesson 6 Session 9

Cost of Capital — Cost of Equity

When a company raises money from its owners (shareholders), that money is not free. Shareholders expect a return in exchange for the risk they take. The minimum return they demand is the cost of equity. This lesson builds the idea from zero and then shows the two classic ways to measure it — the CAPM and the Dividend Growth Model — using real Indian companies.

In this lesson

1. What is the cost of capital? Session 9

A business is funded by two broad sources of money: equity (the owners / shareholders) and debt (lenders such as banks and bondholders). Each source wants to be paid for providing that money. The cost of capital is simply the overall return a company must earn to keep all of its fund-providers happy.

It has three building blocks, which we meet across this lesson and the next:

  • Cost of equity (Ke) — the return shareholders require. This lesson.
  • Cost of debt (Kd) — the after-tax interest lenders require. Next lesson.
  • WACC — the two blended together by how much of each the company uses. Next lesson.
What is "cost of equity"?

The cost of equity is the minimum return equity investors require for putting (and keeping) their money in the company, given its risk. It is the firm's cost because it must earn at least this much for shareholders — and it is the investor's required return. Same number, two viewpoints.

Features of equity shares

Before measuring the cost of equity, understand what an equity share actually is. Six features define it:

FeatureWhat it means
Residual claimEquity holders are paid last — after debt and preference holders. In return they own the residual profits and assets (whatever is left over).
Voting rightsTypically one share = one vote on major corporate decisions.
Discretionary dividendsDividends are not fixed or guaranteed. They are paid only at the board's discretion.
Perpetual capitalEquity has no maturity date; it stays on the balance sheet indefinitely.
Limited liabilityA shareholder's loss is capped at the amount invested — personal assets are safe.
Capital appreciationReturns come mainly through a rising share price, not fixed interest.
💡 Why this makes equity costly

Because equity is paid last (residual), dividends are uncertain, and returns ride on the share price, equity is riskier than debt for the investor. More risk → higher required return. This is exactly why the cost of equity is almost always higher than the cost of debt.

Dual-class and multi-class shares

Normally, one share = one vote, so voting power matches your economic investment. But some companies issue dual-class or multi-class shares: all classes have the same economic (dividend) rights, but different voting rights. Founders keep the high-vote shares and sell low-vote (or no-vote) shares to the public. This lets them raise capital without losing control.

Alphabet (Google) — three classes Session 9

  • Class A (GOOGL): 1 vote/share, publicly traded
  • Class B: 10 votes/share, held only by founders, never publicly traded
  • Class C (GOOG): no votes, publicly traded

Founders Page & Brin hold ~52.7% of the voting power with under 12% of the economic stake.

Meta (Facebook) — two classes Session 9

  • Class A (META): 1 vote/share, publicly traded
  • Class B: 10 votes/share, held almost entirely by Mark Zuckerberg

Zuckerberg holds ~61% of total voting power with only ~13–14% of the economic interest.

ℹ️ The big idea

A small economic stake can translate into majority control — the classic separation of ownership from control. The founders own a little but decide a lot.

2. Two kinds of risk Session 9

To price the cost of equity we must understand what shareholders are being compensated for: risk. Total risk splits into two very different types.

Systematic risk

Also: market risk, non-diversifiable risk.

Affects the entire market/economy. It arises from macroeconomic, political or social forces that no single company controls — and it cannot be diversified away.

Examples: interest-rate hikes, inflation, recession (the 2008 crisis), a pandemic (COVID-19).

Unsystematic risk

Also: specific risk, diversifiable risk.

Affects one company or one industry only. It can be reduced or eliminated by holding a diversified portfolio — one firm's bad news is offset by another's good news.

Examples: a failed acquisition, a labour strike at one plant, a single product recall, an accounting fraud/scandal.

BasisSystematic riskUnsystematic risk
ScopeEntire market / economySpecific company / industry
SourceMacroeconomic, political, socialFirm-specific (management, labour)
Diversifiable?NoYes
Also calledMarket riskSpecific / residual risk
Measured byBeta (β)Std. deviation of firm-specific returns
Priced by CAPM?Yes — investors are compensatedNo — diversified away at no cost
⚠️ Common mistake — only systematic risk is priced

A frequent exam trap: assuming investors are paid for all the risk they carry. They are not. Because unsystematic risk can be diversified away for free, the market refuses to reward you for bearing it. Only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk is priced — and that is exactly the risk beta measures and CAPM charges for.

3. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) Session 9

CAPM turns the idea above into a formula. It says the return an investor requires equals the risk-free return plus a reward for the systematic risk taken (measured by beta):

Cost of equity — CAPM
$$K_e = R_f + \beta \times (R_m - R_f)$$
$K_e$
Cost of equity — the minimum return equity investors require
$R_f$
Risk-free rate — return on a riskless asset (e.g., the 10-year G-Sec)
$\beta$
Beta — sensitivity of the stock to overall market movements
$R_m$
Expected market return — expected return on the broad market index
$(R_m - R_f)$
Market risk premium — the extra return demanded for bearing market risk

Read it in words: start with what you could earn risklessly ($R_f$), then add β "doses" of the market's risk premium. A high-β stock takes a bigger dose and must therefore offer a bigger return.

Two real BSE Sensex stocks, using the India 10-year G-Sec yield ≈ 7.0% as $R_f$ and an assumed market return $R_m = 13\%$ (so the market risk premium is $13\% - 7\% = 6\%$):

Worked example Reliance (β = 0.83) and TCS (β = 1.13) ›
Formula $K_e = R_f + \beta (R_m - R_f)$
Given $R_f = 7\%$, $R_m = 13\%$, so $(R_m - R_f) = 6\%$.
Reliance — working $K_e = 7\% + 0.83 \times 6\% = 7\% + 4.98\% = 11.98\%$
TCS — working $K_e = 7\% + 1.13 \times 6\% = 7\% + 6.78\% = 13.78\%$
Reliance Ke ≈ 11.98%  ·  TCS Ke ≈ 13.78%

Interpretation: Reliance (β = 0.83) is less volatile than the Sensex, so it needs only a modest risk premium. TCS (β = 1.13) is more volatile than the Sensex, so its shareholders demand a higher return. Higher beta → higher cost of equity.

✎ Try it — CAPM. A stock has β = 1.50, with Rf = 7% and Rm = 13%. What is its cost of equity Ke (in %)?

$K_e = 7\% + 1.50 \times (13\% - 7\%) = 7\% + 1.50 \times 6\% = 7\% + 9\% = \mathbf{16\%}$. A β of 1.5 means 1.5 doses of the 6% premium = 9% on top of the risk-free 7%.

Understanding beta (β)

Beta is the heart of CAPM. It measures how much a stock moves relative to the market:

BetaMeaningTypical examples
β = 1Moves in line with the marketBroad index-like stocks
β > 1More volatile than the market (aggressive)Tech, cyclicals
β < 1Less volatile than the market (defensive)FMCG, utilities
β = 0No correlation with the market (theoretical, risk-free-like)—
β < 0Moves opposite the market (rare)e.g., some gold miners

Estimating historical beta

Beta is estimated from past returns in two equivalent ways:

Method 1 — Covariance / Variance
$$\beta = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)}$$
$\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)$
how the stock's returns move together with the market's returns
$\text{Var}(R_m)$
the market's own volatility (variance)
Method 2 — Regression (slope)
$$R_i = \alpha + \beta \times R_m + \varepsilon$$
$\alpha$
alpha — the part of the stock's return unrelated to the market
$\beta$
beta — the slope of the line = systematic risk
$\varepsilon$
epsilon — the error term = firm-specific (unsystematic) risk
Worked example Reliance vs Sensex, 5 monthly returns ›

Monthly returns: compute the mean of each column, take deviations, then cross-products and squared market deviations.

Note: beta varies with the time period, return frequency and benchmark index chosen — which is why different data providers report different betas for the same stock.

Stock alpha (α)

Where beta measures risk, alpha measures skill. Alpha is the excess return an investment earns above what CAPM predicts for its level of risk — it isolates genuine skill from simply taking more risk:

Stock alpha
$$\alpha = R_{\text{actual}} - \big[R_f + \beta (R_m - R_f)\big]$$
$R_{\text{actual}}$
the return the stock/fund actually delivered
$R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$
the return CAPM expected given its risk
AlphaMeaning
α = 0Performed exactly as expected for its risk
α > 0Outperformed its risk-adjusted expectation (added value)
α < 0Underperformed its risk-adjusted expectation
Worked example Active fund vs its CAPM expectation ›
Given Fund B: β = 0.8, actual return = 12%, Rf = 4%, benchmark Rm = 10%.
CAPM expected $= 4\% + 0.8 \times (10\% - 4\%) = 4\% + 4.8\% = 8.8\%$.
Alpha $\alpha = 12\% - 8.8\% = +3.2\%$.
α = +3.2% — the fund beat its risk-adjusted expectation by 3.2 percentage points.

Interpretation: a fund with a lower headline return can still have a higher alpha if it took less risk. Alpha, not raw return, reveals manager skill.

4. The Dividend Growth (Gordon) Model Session 9

CAPM is not the only route to the cost of equity. The Dividend Growth Model (DGM) — also called the Gordon Growth Model — values a share as the present value of all its future dividends, assumed to grow at a constant rate forever:

Share price (Gordon model)
$$P_0 = \dfrac{D_1}{K_e - g}$$
$P_0$
current market price of the share
$D_1$
expected dividend next year $= D_0 \times (1+g)$
$D_0$
the most recent (current) dividend per share
$K_e$
cost of equity (the required return we want)
$g$
constant expected growth rate of dividends

Rearranging to solve for the cost of equity gives the formula we actually use:

Cost of equity — DGM
$$K_e = \dfrac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$

In words: the required return = the dividend yield next year ($D_1/P_0$) plus the growth rate ($g$). You are rewarded both by the cash dividend and by the growing stream behind it.

Worked example HUL: D₀ = ₹53, P₀ = ₹2,209, g = 14.35% ›
Formula $K_e = \dfrac{D_1}{P_0} + g$, with $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$.
Given $D_0 = ₹53$, $P_0 = ₹2{,}209$, $g = 14.35\%$.
Step 1 — D₁ $D_1 = 53 \times (1 + 0.1435) = ₹60.6$.
Step 2 — apply $K_e = \dfrac{60.6}{2209} + 0.1435 = 2.74\% + 14.35\%$.
HUL Ke ≈ 17.1%

Interpretation: HUL's dividend grew from ₹31 (FY2021) to ₹53 (FY2025). The required return breaks into a small 2.74% dividend yield plus a large 14.35% growth component.

Worked example Infosys: D₀ = ₹45, P₀ = ₹1,500, g = 10.7% ›
Given $D_0 = ₹45$, $P_0 = ₹1{,}500$, $g = 10.7\%$.
Step 1 — D₁ $D_1 = 45 \times (1 + 0.107) = ₹49.8$.
Step 2 — apply $K_e = \dfrac{49.8}{1500} + 0.107 = 3.32\% + 10.7\%$.
Infosys Ke ≈ 14.0%

Interpretation: Infosys' dividend grew from ~₹30 (FY2021) to ~₹45 (FY2025), including a special dividend in FY2024. Its required return is 3.32% yield + 10.7% growth ≈ 14.0%.

Use the DGM calculator below to reproduce HUL (D₀ = 53, P₀ = 2209, g = 14.35) and Infosys (D₀ = 45, P₀ = 1500, g = 10.7). Then change the growth rate and watch how sensitive Ke is to g.

⚠️ Common mistake — the g ≥ Ke trap

The Gordon model breaks down whenever g ≥ Ke. Look at $P_0 = D_1/(K_e - g)$: if the growth rate equals or exceeds the required return, the denominator becomes zero or negative, giving an infinite or nonsensical (negative) price. No company can grow its dividends faster than its cost of equity forever. Always sanity-check g against Ke before applying the model mechanically.

5. Estimating the growth rate (g) Session 9

The whole DGM swings on g, so estimating it well matters. Analysts use two standard approaches.

Method 1 — Geometric mean of historical dividends

Historical growth
$$g = \left(\dfrac{D_n}{D_0}\right)^{1/n} - 1$$
$D_0$
the earliest dividend on record
$D_n$
the most recent dividend
$n$
the number of years between them

This reflects the company's actual dividend track record — but it can be distorted by one-off special dividends.

Worked example Geometric growth, FY2021 → FY2025 (n = 4) ›
HUL $g = (53/31)^{1/4} - 1 = (1.71)^{0.25} - 1 \approx 14.35\%$.
Infosys $g = (45/30)^{1/4} - 1 = (1.5)^{0.25} - 1 \approx 10.67\%$.
HUL g ≈ 14.35%  ·  Infosys g ≈ 10.67%

Method 2 — Sustainable growth rate (b × r)

The sustainable growth rate asks: how fast can the company grow using only the profits it reinvests? It multiplies how much it keeps by how well it reinvests:

Sustainable growth
$$g = b \times r$$
$b$
retention ratio $= 1 - \text{payout ratio} = 1 - \dfrac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}}$ — the share of earnings kept, not paid out
$r$
return on equity (ROE) $= \dfrac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Equity}}$ — the return earned on reinvested capital

A low b means a mature firm paying most profits out (e.g. FMCG). A high b means a growth firm reinvesting heavily. A higher, more stable r lets reinvested earnings compound faster, supporting higher g.

Worked example Sustainable g — HUL vs Infosys ›
HUL payout 92.55% → b = 7.45% = 0.0745 ; ROE = 21.55% = 0.2155. $g = 0.0745 \times 0.2155 \approx 1.6\%$.
Infosys payout 68.5% → b = 31.5% = 0.315 ; ROE = 30.8% = 0.308. $g = 0.315 \times 0.308 \approx 9.7\%$.
HUL sustainable g ≈ 1.6% (vs historical 14.35%)  ·  Infosys sustainable g ≈ 9.7% (vs historical 10.67%)

Interpretation: for HUL the two methods diverge sharply — its history includes unusually large payouts, so 14.35% overstates what it can sustainably repeat. For Infosys the two converge (9.7% vs 10.67%): its payout is stable and consistent with its profitability, so the DGM estimate is far more reliable.

✎ Try it — sustainable growth. A company has a payout ratio of 68.5% and ROE of 30.8%. What is its sustainable growth rate g = b × r (in %)? Session 9

Retention b = 1 − payout = 1 − 0.685 = 0.315. Then $g = b \times r = 0.315 \times 0.308 = 0.097 = \mathbf{9.7\%}$. First convert the payout to a retention ratio, then multiply by ROE.

💡 Which g to trust?

When payout is stable (Infosys), both methods converge and the DGM is trustworthy. When payout is volatile or unusually high (HUL), the methods diverge — use judgment, or apply a multi-stage growth model instead of one constant g.

6. CAPM vs the Dividend Growth Model Session 9

These are complementary, not competing tools. Each suits a different situation.

BasisCAPMDividend Growth Model
ApplicabilityAll listed companies (dividend or not)Only dividend-paying companies
Risk measurementExplicit — via betaImplicit / ignored
Data requiredRf, β, RmPrice, expected dividend, growth rate
Key assumptionEfficient marketsConstant, perpetual dividend growth
SensitivityBeta & market-premium estimatesHighly sensitive to the growth rate (g)
Best suited forGrowth/tech & all-sector firmsStable, mature, dividend-paying firms
🔑 Recap — what to remember
  • Cost of equity = the minimum return shareholders require; equity is risky (residual, discretionary dividends) so Ke > Kd.
  • Only systematic risk is priced; unsystematic risk is diversified away for free.
  • CAPM: $K_e = R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$. Reliance (β 0.83) → 11.98%; TCS (β 1.13) → 13.78%.
  • Beta: =1 with market, >1 aggressive, <1 defensive, 0 none, <0 opposite. Alpha = actual − CAPM expected.
  • DGM: $K_e = D_1/P_0 + g$. HUL → 17.1%; Infosys → 14.0%. Breaks down if g ≥ Ke.
  • Growth: geometric $g=(D_n/D_0)^{1/n}-1$ vs sustainable $g = b \times r$ (b = 1 − payout, r = ROE).
